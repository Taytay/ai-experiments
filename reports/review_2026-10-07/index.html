What 239 experiments taught us about categorising transactions, and what to do next

A look back over the whole project: the goal, what is now known, how sure we can be of it, what an outside expert would add, and a short path to a recommendation YNAB could act on. Every number is an aggregate from REPORT.md; no budget data appears here.

VerdictThe goalLeaderboardWhat we learned How sure we areRecommended experimentsNext stepsRepo cleanup

The science questions are mostly answered. For personal, purpose-defined categories, the household's own behaviour, found by retrieval, beats merchant facts stored in the weights. The training data matters more than the architecture. First-time payees are where a model earns its keep: YNAB's rule gets none of them right; the best models get about 57%.

The weak point is that there is one real user. Every real-data claim rests on the owner's budget, which has now been read about 80 times and used to design the synthetic data. It has become a development set. Another point of accuracy on it is worth less than the first measurement on anyone else's budget.

The encoder race is close to done. The cheap encoders (fcr, EmbeddingGemma 2) sit 1.5–2 points behind decider-4B, mostly on first-time payees. With a GPU kept running behind an API, serving cost is set mostly by how many GPUs stay on, not by the model; even unbatched, one H100 scores decider-4B at about 25 ms a transaction, roughly 3.5M a day. Speed is not what holds the product back; real-user accuracy and trustworthy auto-filing are.

The goal, and how well each part is answered

The owner set the research framing on 2026-09-26: understand the mechanics rather than tune a product. Product use weights the metrics. YNAB has over a million users, each with a personal scheme, and one shared model must serve all of them, with no adapter per user. The model should auto-file the very confident transactions and suggest up to three categories otherwise.

Q1 · How LLMs and encoders work as multiple-choice categorisers well answered

A single answer slot over labelled options matches scoring each option separately, at about 1/15 of the cost (§76 Single answer slot readout). Random option labels scale to 255 options (§105). The decision forms by layers 12–15 (§74). One temperature fixes over-confidence, but it does not transfer between models or populations (§116 Thresholds under distribution shift).

Encoders trained on the same episodes match decider on synthetic data and transfer worse to real data. Override training and v5 households closed most of an 8–12 point gap, leaving about 2 points overall and about 5 on first-time payees (§202, §210).

Q2 · The best way to inject knowledge answered, and the answer is surprising

On synthetic data, database episodes store facts, and at scale part of that generalises (§165, §167). On the real budget, merchant knowledge in the weights does not move first-time payees, even for merchants that were trained (§167 Scaling merchant knowledge). Kind lines from lookups are right only 55–65% of the time and net zero or worse (§168, §194).

What moved real numbers was behaviour: similar-payee rows took first-time payees from 37.8 to 51.3 with no retraining (§157), alias rows added more (§158), and other households' filings add 6–7 on first-time payees, though only on synthetic data so far (§169, §198).

Q3 · Inferring unseen or meaningless category names answered on synthetic data

Rename augmentation makes the model nearly indifferent to names: 89–90 on invented names against 90–92 on real ones (§78, §82). Category synonyms cost 0–2 points (§207). On real data the hard cases are a category's first use (15–18% right), person- or purpose-scoped categories (47–53%) and catch-alls (37–39%). None of these has a test of its own yet.

Product goal promising, not established

On the owner's budget the best systems get 74–75% right first, against 57.9% for YNAB's rule, and about 85% within three suggestions, saving 76–77% of the manual work (§161, §162). Auto-filing could cover about 66% of transactions at 90% precision, 45% at 95% and 10–16% at 98%, but those thresholds were chosen on the same budget.

Lessons that generalise

  1. Retrieve behaviour; don't store facts. The payee's own dated rows, alias rows, similar payees and other households' filings moved real and blind numbers. Merchant facts and kind lines did not (§157, §158, §167, §168).
  2. The latest filing matters more than the majority. On the real budget a payee's latest filing beats its majority 43% of the time, against 18% in v4 households (§196). Override training was worth +7.4 for the encoder (§190), and "last category first" is the best simple policy (§162).
  3. Synthetic sets reward fitting the generator. REAL-7 hid forgetting (§151). Synthetic rankings did not predict the owner's ranking (§187, §189). v5 was built to rank like the owner (0.83) and then misranked first-time readers in the blend (§212).
  4. Seed noise on one real budget is 1–2 points (§168). Most single-seed gains from §163–§167 did not survive a second seed.
  5. Fusion needs errors that differ. An encoder is right where decider is wrong on only 4.3–4.6% of items, and no confidence rule finds them (§180, §187, §212).
  6. Choosing settings on the test budget inflates them. Fusion's +1.0–1.5 fell to +0.3 once its settings were chosen on synthetic data (§178).
  7. Real-data hygiene pays. Trim category names: stray spaces cost 7 points (§155). Read label logits in fp32: bf16 ties on 1.7% of items. Offer only the options valid at the time: that fix was worth +2.2 (§189).

Tried and never helped

Prose knowledge and category descriptions (§158); rationales (§164); JSON, YAML and TypeScript formats (§114); interaction layers (§185); prototypes (§183); Pinterest's encoder fixes (§182); longer, structured queries (§201); cold-start examples (§208); the busy-category correction (§209); more LoRA capacity, which forgets world knowledge (§151); distilling the 35B on synthetic data (§136).

How sure we can be: what an expert would flag

  • One real user. There is no variation between users, no held-out-user result (the production metric) and no measure of new users with short histories. The 0.2 gate flipped sign across three blind sets (+4.8, +0.1, −1.3); real users will vary at least as much.
  • The test budget has leaked into the design. The string grammar of real-style households is fitted to the owner's strings (§161), v5 and v6 are tuned to the owner's aggregates (§196, §206), and the payee embeddings were fitted over all payee names, future ones included. "Real-style" gains may partly be "owner-style" gains. No sealed real test exists.
  • The gold labels lean toward the incumbent. Many categories were probably pre-filled by YNAB's own rule and accepted, which flatters the rule on known payees. Label noise and "both answers acceptable" cases are unmeasured.
  • The statistics are thin. The 21k transactions are correlated by month and payee, and no block-bootstrap intervals are reported. Arms are screened on one seed and only winners get a second (winner's curse). The headline systems (74.9, 75.2) are single seeds.
  • Comparisons are not quite like for like. Readers use 21,238 or 19,093 items with different option sets. Latency is measured on four GPU types. decider's 10–13 ms assumes many transactions a day; one a day reads at about 45 ms.
  • The metrics don't match the product yet. Top-1 is the headline, but the product decision is auto-file coverage at a guaranteed precision, using thresholds fitted on other users. That has never been measured on real data.
  • There is no ceiling on real data. Nobody knows how much of the remaining 25% error is knowable from the name and history at all. A strong classical recommender baseline (gradient boosting over recency counts, last category, amount, weekday, crowd prior) was never built.
  • Opportunity cost. About 60 rows (§177–§212) optimised a 33M encoder against stand-ins that kept failing to predict the real ranking, while serving cost doesn't make the encoder necessary at this scale.

Recommended experiments

These merge the internal review with an outside scan of 2023–2026 work (Intuit's Rel-Cat, Plaid, Ramp, Nubank's nuFormer, Visa, Revolut's PRAGMA, conformal methods). Vendor accuracy figures are the vendors' claims, not benchmarks.

Now, on saved scores, with no new data

One frozen scorecard with honest intervals

Every reader and policy on the same 21,238 items and options, with month-block bootstrap intervals and thresholds fitted on the first half of the timeline and read on the second. Declare the owner's budget a development set from here on.

About $5–15 · fixes the like-for-like and statistics problems

Auto-filing with a guaranteed error rate

Conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al., ICLR 2024) picks the auto-file threshold so that the error on auto-filed rows stays at or below α. Conditional conformal (Gibbs, Cherian & Candès) holds that separately for first-time payees. Batch calibration (ICLR 2024) removes per-label bias from decider's option scores at no cost. Report coverage at α = 1%, 2% and 5%, plus variable-size suggestion sets against the fixed top-3.

CPU only · turns "auto-file the very confident" into a number the product can promise

A strong classical baseline and a learned stacker

A gradient-boosted ranker over the rule, the payee's last category, recency-weighted counts, amount, weekday, crowd counts and each model's probabilities. Add Rel-Cat's cascade, where the nearest neighbour answers when similarity is high and the LLM sees only the ambiguous rows. This shows whether the LLM is needed for known payees at all.

Under $5 · never tried

An error audit by the owner, giving a real ceiling

Sample 300 misses (first-time payees, and known payees filed differently). The owner labels each one: knowable from name or history, needs item-level data, depends on purpose or person, label noise, or an acceptable alternative. Also split the scorecard by whether the gold category has been used before (Rel-Cat: 78.6% on used categories against 20.8% on unused ones).

$0 compute, 2–3 owner hours · says where the remaining points can come from

Once more real users exist

A real multi-user test set, sealed

20–100 consenting budgets (YNAB staff, beta users, or an anonymised production sample through the REAL_DATA_SPEC pipeline), split by user, with test users sealed and the read pre-registered the way blind_v3 was. Rehearse on synthetic users through the exact pipeline first. A cheap stopgap: MoneyData (Mendeley) holds about 6,500 real, categorised transactions from one person over seven years. Its licence must be confirmed first; with it, the work would have a second real user next week.

Data and legal work; under $100 compute · the most valuable item on this list

A real crowd line

Aggregate production filings into a bank string → category-name histogram, k-anonymous (at least 10 households) and with no rows, then read the existing crowd-trained models on held-out users. It is worth +6–7 on first-time payees on synthetic data, the largest untested lever.

An aggregation job plus $10–30 of reads

Train on real filings

The decider-4B recipe trained on real training users against synthetic-only, read on the sealed users, two seeds. Real data may also change the capacity answer and make knowledge episodes pay. Add hard negatives from crowd disagreement: the same payee filed under semantically different names, as in Plaid's transaction model.

About $40–80

Learning from corrections within a session

Replay the timeline with a simulated user who accepts a right first suggestion and corrects a wrong one. Measure how soon a correction or a new category changes later predictions. Compare decider's in-context updating with a frozen encoder plus a tiny per-user head refit after each approval (Intuit's personalisation patents). The recommender framing promised this property; it has never been measured.

CPU plus existing reads

Speculative, after the above

Per-user category descriptions as label text

Write a one-line meaning per category from the user's own history ("Kids: school fees, back-to-school shopping") for decider and for the label tower (BYOC, 2023). Prose descriptions failed in §158, but those were generic, not drawn from the user's own filings.

About $10

A masked-category sequence model

Hide the category of one filing and predict it from every row's payee, amount and date plus the visible categories: the transaction-foundation-model idea (nuFormer, TREASURE, PRAGMA) adapted to personal labels. It is only sensible with real multi-user sequences.

$20–50

Payee resolution as a product component

Payee resolution alone takes YNAB's rule from 57.9 to 64.0 (§158). Link payees to Overture places by retrieving candidates and having a small model pick one or NONE (Match, Compare or Select, COLING 2025), shuffling candidate order to measure position bias. This is shippable without any categoriser.

$0–5

Park or stop

v7 or later generators tuned to the owner; further encoder architecture tweaks (§182–§203 all null); the Clef fine-tune; the game task (row 215); EVoC and semantic IDs (row 197); and the REAL-6-era rows 43, 44, 46, 51, 54 and 55.
