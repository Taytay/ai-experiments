Row 177 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · §164 Row numbers and rationalesdone

Numbering past transactions and teaching the model to explain its choice

Why we ran it

After Row 176, the owner asked: "Should we try to draw attention to other rows in the history more ... by having it generate rationale for the category?" They also suggested giving each history row a number, so that an explanation could point to similar rows, "like previous restaurants and that this appears to be a restaurant". The owner also had a doubt: "the model needs to learn to do that itself, and with our real dataset, we won't be able to write rationale for everything".

What we tried

The generator of synthetic households (made-up budgets with known answers) now records why each transaction got its category: a habit, a trip, a person's purpose, a bill, a misfile, and so on. Arm C numbered every past transaction in the prompt. Arm D also trained a short explanation that comes after the answer. The explanation cites rows by number, so it costs nothing when the model is used. Both arms used the same households as the earlier controls.

Example prompt

Transaction: [12] 2024-05-01 | BLUEJAY CAFE #44 | $14.20 | Category: (AH) Restaurants
Transaction: [13] 2024-05-03 | MAPLE GROCERS | $82.10 | Category: (AJ) Groceries
...
Answer: (AH) Restaurants
Because: looks like a cafe; this payee's rows [12], [31]; similar payees [7] Robin Bistro went to Restaurants; habit

Illustrative, invented: the payees and rows are made up. They show the row numbers and the explanation format.

How it did

ArmOwner's budget, first guess rightREAL-6 drill"Changed their mind"
A2: control73.184.620.9
C: row numbers73.386.620.9
D: row numbers + explanations72.483.920.9

Percent of transactions where the model's first guess was right. Single training seed (one training run per arm). "Owner's budget" is the owner's real budget, reported only in aggregate.

What we learned

  • Row numbers are cheap and harmless. They help the older test drills a little, but they are not a lever.
  • Explanations do not teach the model where to look. It learns that from the answers. On real data they cost 0.7.
  • The weak score on "changed their mind" cases was the same in every arm. The problem is in the training households, not in the model's attention.

Questions it raised

  • Can the households be fixed so that misfiles get corrected and changes of habit stick? Later shared-world households in row 183 rebuilt the data.
  • Do small prompt fixes help instead? These became the proposed row 178.

Row 178 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · proposedtodo

Tidying the prompt so misleading look-alikes stay out

Why we ran it

This step is proposed and not yet run. It came out of Row 179. There, a contact-lens retailer's second purchase moved to the right category once the model learned merchant facts. Its first purchase stayed wrong. That prompt showed "similar" payees that were similar only because they all ended in ", INC.".

What we tried

Nothing has run yet. Two fixes are planned:

  • Show "similar payee" rows only when the match is strong enough to pass a threshold.
  • Strip company suffixes such as "INC" or "LLC" before comparing names, so that a shared legal ending does not count as similarity.

Example prompt

Query: CLEARVIEW LENSES, INC.
Similar payees (today): HARBOR FREIGHT LINES, INC. -> Transport
                        NORTHSTAR SHIPPING, INC. -> Transport
After the fix: no similar rows (none above the threshold once ", INC." is removed)

Illustrative, invented: all names are made up. They show how a shared suffix can pull in the wrong neighbours.

Questions it raised

  • How many first-time payees lose their only similar rows under a threshold, and is "no hint" better than a misleading one?
  • Wrong hints turned out to be costly in another form too: wrong "Kind" lines in row 182.

Row 179 · 2026-10-03 · MODEL-28 · §165 A local open merchant databasedone

Building a merchant directory from open sources and teaching it to the model

Why we ran it

Our categoriser missed a well-known contact-lens retailer (1-800 Contacts), and no version of it knew what the company sells. The owner asked for "a database of online retailers and in person merchants that we can embed in the training". They said "Go straight to wikidata", then: "build a local database using this. Then let's try to build that info into weights". It followed Row 177 in the queue.

What we tried

We combined openly licensed sources: Wikidata, Curlie, DMOZ, Overture brands and the Majestic popularity ranks. The result is a local database of 297,050 businesses people pay, each tagged with one of 55 "kinds" (grocery, optician, airline and so on). We then trained the 5,000 most popular merchants into decider-4B, our 4-billion-parameter categoriser. Each merchant came as a bank-statement string to be filed under its kind, seen about 26 times. This arm is called K. We tested kind questions on merchants the model was trained on and on merchants it never saw.

How it did

ReaderKind right, trained merchantsKind right, held-out merchantsOwner's budget, first guess
A2: control62.261.173.1
K: + merchant knowledge (5k)84.077.674.3

Percent. On the owner's budget, K scored 81.0 on known payees and 58.0 on first-time payees. Single seed.

What we learned

  • Merchant facts train in, and they generalise. The model also got better at reading what kind of business a merchant it never saw is.
  • The retailer's 2022 purchase moved from Transport to Medical.
  • On the synthetic test drills, the knowledge cost points where the user's own filing should beat what the name suggests.

Questions it raised

  • Should we teach more merchants, or the same merchants more times? See row 180.
  • Do category group names help? See row 181.
  • Can a lookup line from the database help first-time payees? See row 182.

Row 180 · 2026-10-03 · MODEL-28 · §167 Scaling merchant knowledgedone

More merchants or more repetition: how to scale what the model knows

Why we ran it

Row 179 showed that merchant facts can be trained in. The owner said: "Proceed with scaling the knowledge and proceed as far as you can, learning the best way to inject knowledge".

What we tried

We kept the training compute fixed and changed the trade-off. One arm taught 5,000 merchants about 26 times each. Another taught 20,000 merchants about 6.5 times each. Longer runs (3,200 training steps) taught 20,000 merchants 26 times each, or 48,000 merchants 11 times each. A run of the same length without knowledge served as the control. A tiered test measured how much the model kept for the top 5k merchants, the next tiers, and merchants it never saw. We also checked the owner's budget.

How it did

ReaderKind right, top 5kKind right, 5k-20kOwner's budget, first guessFirst-time payees
A2 (800 steps)62.740.673.157.4
5k x 26 (800)84.051.074.358.0
20k x 6.5 (800)78.461.774.657.5
A2 (3,200 steps)54.436.971.956.6

Percent. Single seed. Row 182 later showed that differences of 1 to 2 points on the owner's budget are within seed noise.

What we learned

  • Reaching more merchants beats repeating the same ones. About 11 exposures keep most of a fact.
  • The real-budget gain came on payees the owner had filed before, not on first-time ones. That held even when the model had been taught the payee's merchant.
  • Knowing what a merchant is did not change which purpose the model filed it under.

Questions it raised

  • How do we connect the fact to the decision? A "Kind:" line in the prompt was tried in row 182.
  • How large is seed noise on the owner's budget? Also measured in row 182.

Row 181 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · §166 Writing categories with their groupdone

Writing each category with its group name

Why we ran it

The owner suggested writing each category the way the budget shows it, group and all: "(AJ) Everyday Expenses: Groceries". In the owner's budget the groups are mostly people, properties and projects, so the group says whose purchase a category is. It ran alongside Row 179.

What we tried

Every category in the list and on every past transaction carried its group name. We read this zero-shot (the existing models, never trained on the new format). We also trained with it: G was the control recipe with group names, and GK was the merchant-knowledge recipe with group names.

Row 182 · 2026-10-04 · MODEL-28, REAL-27 · §168 Kind lines from a lookupdone

Telling the model what kind of business each payee is, in the prompt

Why we ran it

Row 180 showed that merchant knowledge helps payees seen before but not first-time ones. Row 181 made group names the base. The idea was to connect the fact to the decision by writing it into the prompt.

What we tried

We added a "Kind:" line under past transactions and under the transaction being filed. In training, the kinds came from the generator for half the payees, with 8% deliberately wrong. On the owner's budget, they came from a name lookup in our merchant database or in Overture's open places data. The lookup covered 61% of transactions. We hand-checked 200 payees to see how often the lines were right. We also trained a second seed (a second training run with different randomness) to measure noise.

Example prompt

Transaction: 2024-03-09 | MAPLE GROCERS #12 | $58.40 | Category: (AJ) Everyday Expenses: Groceries
  Kind: grocery store
Query: 2024-04-02 | TIDEWATER OPTICAL | $129.00
  Kind: optician

Illustrative, invented: the payees are made up.

How it did

MeasureValue
Kind lines right, from our database49%
Kind lines right, from Overture74%
G, two seeds (mean)73.2 / 74.3 (73.75)
GK, two seeds (mean)74.6 / 72.8 (73.7)
Best line arm (GLK20, Overture lines) vs. GK20 without lines74.3 vs. 74.6

Owner's budget, percent right on the first guess. Kind-line accuracy is per transaction, from a 200-payee hand check (counts only).

What we learned

  • A second training seed moved results 1 to 2 points, so the earlier single-seed gains on the owner's budget were noise. From here on we used two seeds or paired reads.
  • Wrong lines cost 1.5 on first-time payees, because the model trusts them. Right lines added little: the similar-payee rows already say what kind of place a payee is.
  • The owner's categories are purposes. "Restaurant" does not say whether it was a date or a trip.

  • Would a short description beat a kind? Noted as row 184.

Row 183 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27 · §169 Other households' filings as a linedone

Showing how other households filed the same payee

Why we ran it

Lookups by name were unreliable (Row 182). The owner proposed "putting more emphasis on how others categorize a given payee as our 'database' of 'kinds'", using "Synthetic for now".

What we tried

We built a shared synthetic world in which many households shop at the same merchants through a few banks, so they see the same bank strings. For each string we counted the category names other households used. We call this the crowd line. The household being tested was always left out, and the line appeared only when two or more others had filed that string. G3 trained without the line and GC trained with it, two seeds each. We tested on held-out households in a new world (merchants never seen in training) and in the training world.

Example prompt

Query: 2024-07-14 | SQ *BLUEJAY CAFE | $11.75
Others filed this payee as: Restaurants (15), Eating out (7), Coffee (3)

Illustrative, invented: the payee and counts are made up.

How it did

New world, held-out householdsG3, no lineG3, line (untrained)GC, line
All71.567.772.6
Payee filed before74.569.174.8
First-time payee55.159.660.2

Percent right on the first guess, mean of two seeds. The owner's budget has no crowd data. Read without the line, GC scored 72.1 against G3's 73.55.

What we learned

  • Once trained, the line lifts first-time payees by 6 to 7 points, with nothing lost on known payees.
  • Training on many households puts some of this into the weights (+3.4), but the line adds 7 more.
  • Read untrained, the line costs known payees 5: the model follows the crowd over the household's own history.
  • Trained with the line, the model leans on it. It loses 1.5 when the line is missing.

Questions it raised

  • Can the model stop depending on the line? See row 185 and row 188.
  • Can a better encoder group bank strings for the crowd? See row 186.

Row 185 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27 · §171 Dropping the crowd line sometimesdone

Hiding the crowd line part of the time so the model does not depend on it

Why we ran it

In Row 183, the model trained with the crowd line lost 1.5 on the owner's budget, which has no line. In training the line was missing only for rare merchants, so its absence came to mean "a rare merchant".

What we tried

We used dropout: the line was left out of 40% of the training examples that would have had it, so that its absence carries no signal. This arm is called GCD, with two seeds. It had to keep the 6 to 7 point gain on first-time payees when the line is present, and match G3 (no crowd training) when it is absent.

Row 186 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27 · §170 A contrastive payee encoderdone

Training a small model to tell when two bank strings are the same merchant

Why we ran it

The crowd line in Row 183 needs bank strings grouped by merchant. The owner suggested "triples or hard negatives ... cause the clean payee and the corrupted payee to embed very close together".

What we tried

An encoder is a small model that turns text into a list of numbers, so that similar texts get similar numbers. We used contrastive training: show the encoder two renderings of one merchant and teach it to place them close together. A "hard negative", a different merchant whose name starts with the same word, is pushed apart. The model was bge-small, trained on 200,000 such triples from 83,552 synthetic merchants. On the owner's real payees we compared it with Jaro-Winkler (a classic string-similarity score) and with decider's own embedding. The owner's strings were embedded on this machine only.

How it did

Matcher (owner's real strings)Top-1 right payeeRecallFalse alarms on new payees
Jaro-Winkler83.575.423.9%
Decider-4B embedding77.868.513.9%
Contrastive encoder79.961.16.9%

On held-out synthetic merchants the encoder matched 98.7% (81.1 before training). Used to pick similar-payee rows, it cost payees with no alias 0.9 to 1.6.

What we learned

  • It raises the fewest false alarms of any matcher, but it finds fewer true matches.
  • Synthetic accuracy overstates real accuracy.
  • "Same merchant" is not "similar merchant". For a new payee, the prompt needs payees like it, such as another taco shop, which an identity encoder pushes away.

Questions it raised

  • Can the same training pull payees toward categories? See row 187.

Row 187 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §172 A two-tower payee/category encoderdone

Matching transactions straight to categories with an encoder

Why we ran it

Following Row 186, the owner asked: "Could we push categories and payees together embeddings wise using this same mechanism?"

What we tried

We trained a "two-tower" encoder: one encoder reads both sides, a transaction and a category, and training places each transaction near its category. The wrong answers it learned against included another category from the same household. The category text listed the last three payees filed there, using only earlier filings. To read it, we replayed each budget in date order and ranked every category by closeness. There was no large language model involved.

Example prompt

Transaction: SQ *BLUEJAY CAFE | $11.75 | Saturday
Category:    Everyday Expenses: Eating out; recently: Robin Bistro, Maple Deli, Bluejay Cafe

Illustrative, invented: the two texts the encoder compares. All names are made up.

How it did

ReaderHeld-out households: first guess / top 10Owner's budget: first guess / top 10
bge-small, untrained32.4 / 74.740.7 / 76.7
Two-tower, trained53.0 / 91.847.2 / 78.2

Percent. Decider's own top 10 holds about 90% of the right answers on the owner's budget.

What we learned

  • In the synthetic world, training ties payees to purposes well.
  • On the owner's budget it gains less. Three recent payees describe a category of people, trips and quirks poorly, and a single match cannot see time context such as a trip.
  • As a shortlist for decider it would drop 12 points of right answers, so it is not used that way.

  • What if the encoder compared transactions with the household's own past transactions instead? See row 194.

Why we ran it

Item dropout in Row 185 halved the cost of a missing crowd line. On a budget with no crowd data, though, the line is missing everywhere. The same is true for a new market, a bank nobody else uses, or a lookup outage.

What we tried

In this arm, GCDH, 30% of the training households never had the line at all. The rest still had it removed from 40% of their examples. We trained two seeds and read them on synthetic sets with and without the line, and on the owner's budget without it. The goal was G3's 73.55 on the owner's budget, while keeping the 6 to 7 point gain on first-time payees.

Row 189 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §173 Two-hop similar-payee selectiondone

Finding look-alike payees by what other people do with them

Why we ran it

When a payee is new to a household, the model is shown a few of the household's own earlier payees that look similar, so it can see where "things like this" go. Until now "similar" meant similar spelling. The owner proposed a second route through other people's behaviour: "payee X is often categorized as category clusters x, y, and z, and clusters x, y, and z are the way payees a, b, and c are often categorized." Row 187's two-tower encoder (one model that turns payees and categories into vectors, so matching ones sit close together) had been too weak to file transactions itself, but its category side could group categories from different households.

What we tried

We embedded every category of 800 synthetic households (its name plus the three payees last filed in it) and grouped them into 64 purpose clusters, so "Dining out", "Eating out" and "Takeout & restaurants" land together. Each bank string then got a profile: how many other households filed it in each cluster. The household's own earlier payees with the most similar profile became the similar rows, blended with the spelling ranking. This is two hops: payee to purposes, purposes back to payees. Nothing was retrained and the prompt format did not change. Two decider models read the sets, two seeds each.

Example prompt

Payee: SQ *HARBOR NOODLE 4471 | $23.40 | Sat
Similar earlier payees:
  Pho Lantern        | $31.10 | Dining Out
  Golden Wok Takeout | $18.75 | Dining Out
  Rosie's Diner      | $42.00 | Date Night
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: the three rows were chosen because other households file all four strings into the same purpose clusters, not because they are spelled alike.

How it did

Synthetic set (% right on first guess, mean of 2 seeds)Own rows onlySpelling-similar rowsBehaviour-similar rows
New world, first-time payees (G3)55.160.762.5
New world, first-time payees (GCD)54.260.662.5
New world, first-time, 50+ other households (GCD)58.764.066.8
Training world, first-time payees (GCD)58.862.665.2
New world, all items (GCD)71.572.372.8

Known payees did not move (74.4 to 74.6). Payees with no crowd profile (0-1 other households) were unchanged, as expected.

What we learned

  • Behaviour-chosen rows lift first-time payees 1.5 to 2.6 points over spelling-chosen rows, and about 7 over none, on both seeds and both worlds.
  • The gain is largest where many households filed the string (+2.4 to +3.1 at 50+).
  • The crowd's knowledge enters as the household's own rows, which the model already reads, so nothing breaks when crowd data is missing.

Questions it raised

  • Do behavioural rows add to the crowd line, or does the line already carry the same news? (row 192)
  • Could the purpose clusters become a shared code for categories? (row 197)
  • Could a merchant's vector borrow from its neighbours in this graph? (row 198)

Row 190 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27 · §175 Crowd line on clean keysdone

Rebuilding the synthetic world with two bugs fixed, then measuring the crowd line again

Why we ran it

The crowd line tells the model how other households filed the same bank string. Row 185 and row 188 had trained models to use it while coping with its absence. Two generator bugs then came to light: one renamed about 0.3% of merchant strings, and another merged about 5% of crowd lines that should have stayed apart. The owner asked to rebuild the world under new names rather than patch the old one.

What we tried

We built a "v4" world with both fixes, with new crowd tables, training households and test sets. Three arms of decider-4B were trained, two seeds each: G4 never sees a crowd line; GCD4 sees it on 60% of training items (it is dropped from the other 40%); GCDH4 also hides it from whole households. We read them on held-out synthetic households, with and without the line, and on the owner's real budget, which has no crowd data and so never shows the line.

Example prompt

Payee: TST* CEDAR SMOKEHOUSE | $38.12 | Fri
Others filed this payee as: Restaurants (15), Eating out (7)
Your earlier rows: ...
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: the second line is the crowd line, counts of other households' filings for the same bank string.
