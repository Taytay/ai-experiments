Row 163 · 2026-10-02 · REAL-27 · §155 First results on real datadone

The first real data: replaying eleven years of the owner's budget

Why we ran it

Everything so far was synthetic. The owner offered their own YNAB budget: every approved, categorised transaction is the gold answer, and each is run in date order through decider with only earlier transactions in the prompt. The data stays out of git; only summaries are reported. The model was the recipe settled through row 140.

What we tried

21,238 transactions from 2015 to 2026, scored privately on Modal with nothing stored. Each prompt lists the categories offered that day (median 96, against 10 to 20 for synthetic users), a shared block of 24 earlier rows, up to 6 of the payee's own earlier rows, then the transaction. Readers: the recipe's decider-4B and decider-4B zero-shot, plus a "wide history" arm. Baselines: YNAB's rule (the category used in 2 of the payee's last 3 transactions, else the last; nothing for a new payee) and the payee's last category.

- (pe) Kid A: activities
... (about 96 categories)

...

Category: (

  • A pipeline bug: 27 category names had stray spaces. Real data needs name normalisation.

Row 164 · 2026-10-02 · MODEL-26 · proposedtodo

Cloudflare's Clef decision models and their calibration-focused fine-tuning

Why we ran it

The owner asked to try Cloudflare's newly announced Clef models and their RL fine-tuning framework, as row 151 did for strands-decider. Clef uses a frozen Qwen backbone with a "joint head" that scores every allowed choice at once, trained with label-smoothed cross-entropy plus a Brier loss for calibration.

What we tried

Proposed. (a) Clef and Clef-flash zero-shot in their own code, checked on easy items first, then on blind_v1 and v2 with the other-users line, beside decider-4B and strands. (b) Clef-flash fine-tuned on our episodes (head, then head plus LoRA) with their loss, against our recipe. (c) Their RL framework (RLCD) is partnership-only: ask for access, or stand in with our own calibrated RL loop. Both bases must pass the open-licence check first; Clef (27B) needs an H200.

Questions it raised

  • Does their calibration loss (label smoothing + Brier) help our auto-filing? The same loss is folded into the proposal of row 168.

Row 165 · 2026-10-02 · REAL-27 · §156 The recommended system on real datadone

The full recommended system and its confidence gate on the real budget

Why we ran it

Row 163 tested one reader. The owner asked next for the confidence gate: the whole recommended system from the synthetic work, unchanged, on the same real budget.

What we tried

decider-4B (recipe) reads every transaction. For first-time payees, the untrained decider-35B-A3B's probabilities are multiplied in. For known payees, YNAB's rule goes first unless the model is at least 0.2 more confident in another category (the "gate"). Lists hold at most three suggestions. With one household and no other users, probabilities were calibrated on alternate halves of the timeline. Also reported: "work saved" against filing by hand, and how much could be auto-filed at a precision target.

Row 166 · 2026-10-02 · REAL-27 · §157 Similar payees by embeddingdone

Showing the model rows from similar payees, and the trained 35B

Why we ran it

Two owner requests after row 165: try the trained 35B (from row 125), and "couldn't we favor the payees that embed closer to the payee about to be inferred?", using the payee embeddings of row 157.

What we tried

Every payee name in the budget (5,811) was embedded privately with the recipe's decider-4B. After the payee's own rows, each prompt gets up to 6 rows from the nearest earlier payees (at most 2 per payee). No retraining. Readers: decider-4B recipe and the trained decider-35B-A3B, each with and without the similar rows, and the system card.

Row 167 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · §158 Payee resolution and aliasesdone

Prompt variants and payee aliases on the real budget

Why we ran it

Building on row 166, the owner asked for four things: describe each category by its recent payees and each payee by its recent categories; report whether the right category is in the top 5 or top 10; guarantee that spelling variants of one store (a short name and a coded statement string) show each other's examples; and measure how much probability goes to categories a payee never uses.

What we tried

On row 166's best 4B prompt, no retraining: DESC (each category followed by its three latest payees), PLINE ("Earlier you filed this payee as: A (12), B (3)"), and aliases: payee names cleaned and compared by character n-grams; close matches (cosine ≥ 0.7) get up to 6 of their rows shown, and similar payees are ranked by fusing embedding and n-gram neighbours. A no-model baseline: YNAB's rule run over a payee and its aliases together.

Row 168 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-28 · proposedtodo

Rewarding "might be" and penalising "definitely isn't"

Why we ran it

Row 167 measured how much probability the model leaves on categories a payee is never filed under. The owner suggested rewarding the model for knowing which categories something might be versus definitely isn't, or for suggesting a category that is ever used, even in the future.

What we tried

Proposed; it needs real training data from several consenting budgets, with users held out. Add to the one-slot loss: (a) a penalty on probability given to categories the user never files that payee under; (b) partial credit for any category the user ever files it under, past or future (a training-only signal); (c) the ranked-list cost (the work-saved scale) as a differentiable expected cost; and Clef's label smoothing plus Brier loss for calibration. Judge by right first, top 3/5/10, MRR, probability on never-used categories and work saved, on held-out users and later months. No RL loop is needed: the readout gives the whole distribution in one pass.

Questions it raised

  • Waits on multi-user real data.

Row 170 · 2026-10-03 · MODEL-27 · proposedtodo

Shrinking the 35B's payee knowledge into a smaller model

Why we ran it

Row 166 showed the 35B knows more payees than the 4B, at about 1.6 times the serving cost. The owner asked to distil the 35B down, while training on big real data, so a smaller model keeps the general payee knowledge.

What we tried

Proposed, three routes: (a) expert pruning of decider-35B-A3B (route many real transaction prompts through it, drop or merge the rarely used experts, measure first-time-payee accuracy against size); (b) logit distillation of the 35B (with similar-payee rows) into decider-4B on large real data (it found nothing on synthetic data, where the 35B cannot know invented merchants); (c) FP8 or int4 quantisation of the pruned model. Compared with row 166's 35B and 4B on held-out real users.

Questions it raised

  • Row 173 took the other route to the 35B's knowledge at 4B cost: better training data. Its 4B passed the trained 35B on first-time payees.

Row 171 · 2026-10-03 · DATA-8 · §159 Matching payees to bank stringsdone

Matching new bank strings to known payees, on real strings labelled by YNAB

Why we ran it

Row 167 found a third of "first-time" payees were known payees spelled differently. The owner: "If we were better at just doing payee cleansing alone, we'd be better at all of this", and asked for classic fuzzy-matching tools, noting that they sometimes clean up payees and sometimes don't. It replaces the synthetic plan of row 160 with real gold.

What we tried

YNAB stores each imported transaction's raw bank string with the payee it was assigned: 18,562 transactions, 7,859 distinct strings. Replayed in date order: when a string first appears, each matcher looks it up among earlier strings, and should either find the right payee or abstain if the payee is new. Matchers: exact cleaned string, first word, consonant skeleton, Jaro-Winkler (a string similarity that rewards a shared prefix), token-set ratio, character n-grams, the decider embedding, rank fusion and boosted trees. Thresholds set on the earlier half, read on the later half.

Example prompt

Payee as it appears on a bank statement: SQ *BLUEJAY CAFE 0412
In one word, the kind of spending:

Illustrative, invented: the text the decider embedding reads for one raw string; the other matchers compare strings directly.

How it did

Matcher (later half)top-1 right payee %F1 %false alarms on new payees %
exact cleaned string43.355.16.0
first word79.063.350.7

What we learned

  • Jaro-Winkler finds the payee most often; the embedding raises the fewest false alarms.
  • The gold under-merges: about 17 of 25 sampled "false alarms" were the same merchant under two payees the owner never merged. So combinations trained on this list learn to doubt true matches.

Row 172 · 2026-10-03 · MODEL-28 · §160 Looking payees up in Overturedone

Looking payees up in an open database of 81 million places

Why we ran it

With the matching tools from row 171 in hand, the owner asked whether the large free Overture points-of-interest database could teach us about merchants: look an input payee up in it and inject a description at inference time. On synthetic data, a database "Kind:" line had lifted first-time payees a lot.

What we tried

Overture places (81.5M places, open licences) reduced to 296,375 brands and 12.4M US place names, each with its most common category. Each payee was matched by brand or place name (Jaro-Winkler with strict word rules; person-to-person, check and transfer strings never matched). Matched first-time payees (or every matched payee) got a "Kind: <category>" line under the transaction, on row 167's alias prompt, with the recipe's decider-4B, no retraining.

Training on synthetic households built to look like real ones

Why we ran it

Started as the second half of row 172: put Overture merchant facts into the weights. It changed while being built. Brands cover only 7 to 9% of the owner's first-time-payee transactions, and the owner stressed that their categories are purposes (people, trips, phases of life), not merchant types, and that corrupted payees should look the way their budget shows.

What we tried

From public data plus aggregates of the owner's budget (no literal text stored): a bank-string grammar measured on 7,368 raw strings (prefixes, reference codes, truncation widths); a 60-kind merchant taxonomy from MCC codes and Overture; measured category style and routing (time decides: the payee's nearest-in-time filing predicts 77.1% for multi-category payees); a pool of 61,141 real merchants; and invented households with people, pets, trips, holidays, life phases, person-to-person memos and 3% misfiles. 40,000 training episodes; a 9,000-item test set on held-out merchants. The recipe was trained with 25% or 50% of each batch from these households.

Row 174 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · proposedtodo

Confirm and repair the real-style training

Why we ran it

Row 173 rested on one seed and had a clear cost: on blind_v2, items where a user moves a payee to a new category for good fell from 42.6 to 18.6. The likely cause is the households' 3% random misfiles, which teach the model to ignore an isolated recent change.

What we tried

Proposed: two more seeds of the 25% real-style arm; households where a user re-routes a payee for good and corrects misfiles, instead of random misfiles; a 15% share if misleading-name scores stay down; then the recommended system without the 35B on the owner's budget, blind_v1 and v2, and the real-style test set, with the sealed blind_v3 read once at the end.

Questions it raised

  • Can the time-routing gains be kept while recovering "changed their mind"?

Row 175 · 2026-10-03 · REAL-27 · DATA-8 · §162 Combining the rule and the modeldone

Blending YNAB's simple rule with the model

Why we ran it

The owner noted that YNAB's rule ("the category used in 2 of the payee's last 3 transactions") is very strong, and asked for the best rule of thumb for combining it with the model, so the model enhances something that already works. They also asked how well the new model from row 173 recognises two corrupted strings as the same payee, on the benchmark from row 171.

What we tried

Offline, from stored scores of row 173's reader on the owner's budget (aggregates only): list policies of at most three suggestions, such as the rule first, the payee's last category first, past categories inserted at position 2 or 3, all past categories first (by recency, count or model), the 0.2 gate, and fuzzy aliases. Thresholds were tuned on the earlier half and read on the later half. Separately, the new reader's embedding in row 171's payee-matching replay.

How it did

Row 176 · 2026-10-03 · DATA-8 · runningdoing

Teaching the model to clean up payee strings

Why we ran it

Row 175 showed that the model's embedding still finds the right payee less often than Jaro-Winkler, and that the rule only helps if payees are recognised precisely. The owner asked whether the new real-style dataset from row 173 keeps the "behind the scenes" truth (the original payee before corruption), and suggested training the model to clean up payees as one of its tasks. Row 171 supplies the real benchmark.

What we tried

The real-style generator knows the true merchant behind every corrupted string. The plan in PLAN.md is recognition episodes ("which earlier payee is this new bank string, or a new one?") with candidates from Jaro-Winkler, n-grams and the embedding, plus hard negatives, mixed in at about 10%. What has been built and launched so far (job list r176) is two arms of the recipe with 25% real-style episodes: a regenerated episode set (v2), and v2f, where each history row carries field lines taken from the generator's ground truth (Clean payee, Via, Ordered from, Memo, Location, Store, Reference). With a "field loss", the model is trained to write that whole block itself, including which fields are present. Both are scored on the blind sets, invented names, misleading names and overrides; then the owner's labelled raw strings (target: beat 83.5% top-1 with fewer false alarms) and the last-category-first rule. Results pending.

From realstyle_v2f_train.jsonl (an invented household): the field lines under each history row are what the field loss teaches the model to generate.

What we learned

  • Nothing yet: the run is in progress.
