REAL-6, the main synthetic test set, already had "coined" category names (made-up words like "Grendo"). But those words came from a list of 24 that the training users share, so a model could have memorised them. The row was first set up for the arms of row 58, then re-scoped on 2026-09-27 to the readers that led by then: Qwen3.5-2B trained with database episodes (row 78), the rename-trained Qwen3.5-2B (row 74) and the decider one-slot readers (row 80).

build_real6_novel.py wrote real6_v1_novel: every REAL-6 item with each user's category names replaced by a fresh three-syllable word, used consistently in the category list, the labelled example rows ("shots") and the answer options. Row 62 · 2026-09-26 · REAL-19 · §57 Novel merchants from POI datadone

Real businesses nobody has filed: can the name alone tell the model what they are?

Why we ran it

Every REAL-6 merchant has a database record, and its made-up names mean nothing. So REAL-6 could not ask whether a categoriser can work out an unknown business's kind from its name. A second worry: does training a database into the weights (the database episodes of row 58) hurt guessing about merchants outside it? The owner suggested building the set from Overture, an open map database of real places.

What we tried

1,198 real US businesses from Overture that appear in no database and no user history. Each standard category got 40 independents whose name names their kind ("Chappaqua Dentistry"), 40 with no category word ("Bark N' Bubbles") and 20 chains. Each item went to one REAL-6 user with that user's 24 example rows, in three renderings: obscure (truncated like a bank statement), full name inside bank noise, and clean. Readers were row 58's 3B models (no database, database episodes, record-in-prompt) and the untrained model. Some arms also got a one-line record built from Overture's category. A blind Claude Opus pass on 60 items gave a strong-reader ceiling.

Row 63 · 2026-09-26 · EVAL-9 · §59 Unified scorecard baselinedone

One scorecard for every run, and a no-model baseline that is hard to beat

Why we ran it

In the product, a prediction is used two ways: auto-file the ones the model is extremely sure of, suggest a short list for the rest. Plain accuracy against random chance measured neither. This row built the shared scorecard on the calibration work of row 49 and the novel-merchant runs of row 62.

What we tried

ai_experiments.scorecard reports:

  • top-1 (right first) and top-3 (right in the top three);
  • "bits left": how much uncertainty remains about the answer, after one temperature fitted on other users;
  • coverage: the share the model could auto-file at 95% or 98% precision;
  • a ladder of baselines with no model, from the user's most-used category up to a cascade: the user's own past label for the merchant, else other users' labels, else the most-used category;
  • "skill": the share of the remaining headroom the model wins over the best baseline.

Every REAL-6 and novel-merchant run was re-read with it. CPU only.

Row 64 · 2026-09-26 · REAL-20 · §60 Label induction basicsdone

Label induction: when can a model work out what a made-up category name means?

Why we ran it

Row 62 found that coined category names were the gap between the 3B model and a strong reader. This row takes that gap apart one factor at a time and reads it with the scorecard of row 63.

What we tried

300 real businesses with descriptive names, each under 12 conditions. The query's category is renamed to a coined word, two other categories are coined too, and the 24 examples are other businesses. The conditions vary:

  • how many examples carry the gold word (0 to 8);
  • what those examples are like (same kind as the query, or opaque names);
  • how many categories are coined;
  • a "decoy": one business of the query's own kind filed under another category.

Version 1 turned out to be solvable by elimination, so v2 adds three coined categories with no examples. Readers: untrained Qwen2.5 3B, 7B and 14B; three trained 3Bs; and blind Opus in a Latin square.

Row 66 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-21 · §73 Facts versus skilldone

Does training a database in teach facts or a general skill?

Why we ran it

Database episodes are training examples built from database records, each asking the model to file a known merchant. Row 59 showed they store thousands of merchants. Row 62 saw them add +5 on merchants outside the database, which hinted at a general "this kind of business goes here" skill. REAL-6's merchants are synthetic, so the real places of POI-1 (row 65) made the cleaner test.

What we tried

Half of POI-1's test places (1,001, chosen by a hash of their Overture id) became the injected database. Qwen3.5-2B was trained with fold 0's users held out, once without and once with database episodes, for 800 and 1,600 steps. Each database place appears about 50 times. The test used the plain layout, so nothing in the prompt said what kind a place is. The gain on in-database places is facts plus skill; the gain on held-out places is skill alone.

Example prompt

Transaction: Sky Pool, Virginia Beach VA | $47.28 | Sat
Category: Gudol
[...]
Transaction: WORD-FM, Green Tree PA | $310.37 | Thu
Category:

The plain POI-1 layout: the place's kind is never stated, so only memorised facts or the name can help.

How it did

Arm (fold 0)In database, % right firstHeld outFacts (difference of gains)
No database episodes, 800 steps53.457.0–
Database episodes, 800 steps74.757.4+20.9 [+12.0, +29.7]

What we learned

  • The weights store the particular places: +21 on places in the database, 0 to 3 on places held out.
  • The stored fact matters most where the user has filed nothing of that kind (+28).
  • Longer training turns neither facts into skill nor adds facts; about 50 exposures per place is enough.
  • Train in the places you will see often, and retrieve the rest.

Row 67 · 2026-09-27 · MODEL-8 · §74 Where decisions form in weightsdone

Probing inside the model: where the decision forms and where injected facts live

Why we ran it

Row 64 asked whether the model separates "same kind" from "same category" internally. More broadly, MODEL-8 asked where the categoriser decides and what database episodes write into the weights. It ran after the scorecard of row 63, on the Apache-2.0 Qwen3.5-2B base adopted in row 78.

What we tried

Every REAL-6 prompt went through Qwen3.5-2B in three states: untrained, trained without a database, and trained with database episodes. At each layer we fitted a "probe" (a simple linear classifier) on the hidden state at two points: the last token of the query's bank string, and the final "Category:" cue. The probes were trained on merchants some user had labelled. They were then read on the 60 "DB-only" merchants, whose category reaches the model only through database episodes.

Example prompt

Categories: Apparel & Eating out & Car, Gadgets & Food shopping, Quorra, [...]
[... 24 labelled rows ...]
Transaction: SFWY*7256 | $105.90 | Thu      <- probe 1: last token of the bank string
Category:                                   <- probe 2: the answer cue

An ordinary REAL-6 prompt with no record. The two probe positions are marked.

How it did

Probe accuracy, DB-only merchants (%)UntrainedTrained, no databaseTrained, database episodes
At the bank string, layer 15133191
At the "Category:" cue, layer 12313699
At the cue, layer 153138100

What we learned

  • Untrained, the base model barely commits to a spending category.
  • Training writes the category into the merchant's representation mid-network: readable from layer 12 to 15.
  • Database facts land in the same directions as the model's learned merchant knowledge, since a probe trained on other merchants reads them. One representation of "what this merchant is" gets filled from histories or the database alike.

  • No follow-up row was opened from this. The probes explain where the 72.5 to 86.9 gap from database episodes lives.

Row 68 · 2026-09-27 · MODEL-9 · §72 Encoder confidence objectivesdone

Which training loss gives a small encoder trustworthy confidence?

Why we ran it

Row 53 built a small encoder categoriser: ModernBERT-large with one scored [MASK] slot per category, following Laya's layout. For auto-filing, its confidence has to be trustworthy. The owner noted that Jev, a published decision model, trains with a loss that accounts for confidence.

What we tried

The row 53 encoder (1,500 steps, no record), on all four folds of REAL-6 and POI-1, with four losses:

  • cross-entropy (the baseline);
  • label smoothing 0.1;
  • log score plus spherical score (the pair behind Laya's RL training, here with its exact gradient);
  • cross-entropy plus Brier score.

Each was read by ECE (expected calibration error: the gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy), AURC (mean error across all coverage levels; lower is better) and coverage at 98%. Soft targets from a teacher and multiple-choice pre-training were planned but not run.

Example prompt

[CLS] instruction [SEP] [MASK] category 1 [MASK] category 2 ... [SEP]
query, 24 shots as "statement -> category" [SEP]

The encoder layout (schematic). Each [MASK] is scored, and a softmax runs over the options.

How it did

REAL-6, four foldsTop-1 %ECE raw (points)ECE after temperatureAURC %Auto-filed at 98% (achieved)
Cross-entropy56.727.85.317.112.9 (97.4)
Label smoothing56.613.29.520.62.8 (69.7)
Log + spherical56.425.55.218.23.8 (95.6)
Cross-entropy + Brier58.824.75.117.311.1 (96.2)

What we learned

  • The loss does not buy calibration. One temperature fitted on other users does (ECE about 5 for every loss).
  • Label smoothing is a trap: it looks calibrated raw but ranks its confident answers worse, so auto-filing collapses.
  • At 57% accuracy there are few items to be sure of. Confidence is limited by accuracy, not by the loss.

Questions it raised

  • Confidence objectives are better tested on the stronger one-slot decoders of rows 79 and 80.

Row 71 · 2026-09-26 · MODEL-12 · §65 Hop limits in reasoningdone

Hop limits: how many links of a chain can a model follow?

Why we ran it

The owner asked (2026-09-26): given "a = b, b = c, …", how many hops can a model follow in one pass? Encoders and decoders, short and long names. Can "thinking" steps, looping layers or diffusion denoising raise the limit? Categorising is itself a short chain: query, similar example, its label. The encoder came from row 53.

What we tried

An item set of four shuffled chains of k links (k = 1 to 12) with six options, so chance is 17%. v1 leaked the answer through its options; a control caught this, and v2 fixed it. Readers:

  • untrained Qwen2.5 0.5B to 14B, one pass and with a chain of thought;
  • ModernBERT-large in row 53's layout trained on k ≤ 3 or ≤ 12, plus a looped version, labelled lines with a hop loss, and "scratch rounds" (each hop written back into the input);
  • Huginn (recurrent depth);
  • diffusion models LLaDA, Dream and LFM2.5.

Example prompt

Each line below says that two names are the same thing. Links chain together: if a = b and b = c, then a = c. Follow the links from the start name until the chain ends.

- dish = renewed
- favored = vivid
- collects = jumps
- renewed = controls
- controls = destroys
[... 7 more lines ...]

Start at dish. Where does the chain from dish end?

k = 3: dish → renewed → controls → destroys.

How it did

ReaderHop limit (every k up to it ≥ 80%)
Qwen2.5 3B / 7B / 14B, one pass1
Qwen2.5-14B, chain of thought5
Encoder trained k ≤ 34
Encoder, labelled lines / looped (r = 12)5

What we learned

  • One pass follows as many hops as it was trained on, plus one or two. Untrained decoders follow one.
  • Writing each hop back and re-reading removes the limit, as a chain of thought does.
  • Looping, labelling and a hop loss each add about one hop.
  • Untrained diffusion and recurrent models fail, except Dream-7B, whose denoising steps act like a chain of thought.

Questions it raised

  • Does a hop-trained encoder transfer to categorising? Row 76.
  • Make the categoriser's task one hop instead of two by stating each place's kind: the "Kind:" lines of row 72.

Row 72 · 2026-09-26 · POI-1 · §67 Kind lines in the promptdone

Tell the model what kind of place each example is

Why we ran it

Row 70 proposed that POI-1's seen kinds are a retrieval problem. The hop study (row 71) suggested why the model struggles. With only a record for the query, filing a place takes two hops: learn the query's kind, then spot which examples share it. One pass follows about one untrained hop. The base recipe is row 65's.

What we tried

The row 65 recipe (3B, fold 0 held out, 800 steps, rename 0.5) was trained and scored in several layouts:

  • plain;
  • a record line for the query ("WORD-FM is listed as a radio station");
  • kind-retrieved examples ("kshots");
  • a "Kind:" line under every example and the query, which makes matching one hop;
  • combinations of these.

Untrained 3B and 14B were scored too.

Row 74 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-20 · §71 Empty categories and decoysdone

Why we ran it

Row 64 found the models copy the single nearest example (a decoy) and get drawn to coined categories that have no examples. Row 70's hypothesis 4 said training could fix both. It ran on the open Qwen3.5-2B base of row 78.

What we tried

Qwen3.5-2B trained on REAL-6 users (fold 0 held out) with rename 0.5, all-label loss and 200 steps, in four arms: rename alone; + EMPTY (one to three coined categories with no examples added to half the episodes); + DECOY (in 30% of episodes a same-kind example is relabelled to an unrelated category, with no loss on that label); and both.
