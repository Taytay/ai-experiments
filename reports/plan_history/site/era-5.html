Row 49 · 2026-09-23 · STAT-4 · §50 Calibration and auto-applydone

Can the categoriser's confidence decide when to file automatically?

Why we ran it

A production categoriser needs a rule for when to apply a label without asking. The "Jev review" (thirteen memos on related decision models, collected at the owner's request) found raw model probabilities over-confident, fixable with one fitted temperature, and the right temperature depending on the task type. The audit's groups (row 45) and row 47's longer-trained adapters made the measurement possible.

What we tried

CPU only, from saved per-option scores. A softmax over the user's categories under two scoring rules (mean log-probability per token, and the sum); one temperature per arm, fitted on other users (four folds); calibration error (ECE), Brier score, and the share of items that can be auto-applied at 95% precision with the threshold chosen on other users. Arms: untrained base, no-DB SFT at 200 and 800 steps, record-in-prompt SFT, retrieved record, and row 42's held-out folds.

How it did

arm (sum rule)temperature% rightECE after tempering (points)auto-applied at 95% target (realised precision)
untrained Instruct, no record2.3832.19.54.7% (89.1)
SFT no DB, 200 steps1.8057.33.031.6% (94.6)
SFT no DB, 800 steps2.7972.13.955.8% (95.0)
SFT + record1.4490.33.282.3% (95.4)
no DB, 800 steps, new users2.8869.61.947.8% (94.5)
SFT + record, new users2.2081.34.760.4% (93.3)

What we learned

  • Serve confidences from the sum rule; one temperature per arm then calibrates every categoriser (ECE 2 to 4). The record arm is nearly calibrated as trained; the no-record arm is over-confident, more so the longer it trains.
  • For a new user with the record, about 60% of transactions can be filed automatically at 93 to 95% precision.
  • A temperature borrowed from another arm ruins the probabilities (ECE up to 25) but barely moves the operating point; pick thresholds per arm on held-out users.
  • Confident errors concentrate in categories the user split arbitrarily: 48% of those auto-applied at 16% precision.

Questions it raised

  • Can the model learn to say "needs review"? (row 52)
  • Not done: per-user temperatures, a calibration term in training (later explored for encoders in row 68), the encoder arms.

Row 50 · 2026-09-27 · EVAL-8 · §75 Option order effectsdone

Does the order of the options change the answer?

Why we ran it

The Jev review showed models flipping answers when options are reordered. As first written (after row 41 and row 45) it would permute the category list for the per-option scorers. By 2026-09-27 the leading readers were "one-slot" models (row 79, row 80) that pick a letter label from a lettered option list, which are exposed to position and letter bias, so the row was re-scoped to them.

What we tried

decider-2B and Qwen3.5-2B one-slot readers, re-scored on fold 0 of REAL-6 and of POI-1 (a benchmark of real places) with every item's options shuffled under three seeds; the category list in the prompt unchanged. Measured: accuracy per order, the share of items whose answer changes in any order, and the four-order average.

Counterfactual twin examples to make the model read its evidence

Why we ran it

Across the Jev review, training on decision data usually raised in-distribution accuracy and lost on held-out items. One exception, Nimble's base/counterfactual pairs (two contexts that differ in one fact that flips the label), transferred. Our generator knows the true labels, so such pairs need no verifier. It needs row 42's held-out folds to measure transfer.

What we tried

Planned: for each training episode a sibling where either the history files the query's merchant under another of the user's categories, or the record names another category's products, with the label recomputed; pairs against an equal number of unpaired episodes at the same token budget; no-DB and record arms; scored on held-out folds with a "both twins right" accuracy, and on row 43's set when it exists.

Questions it raised

  • Not run as of 2026-10-03.

Row 52 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-14 · §79 Trained abstentiondone

Teaching the model to answer "not listed here"

Why we ran it

Row 49 found the categoriser confident and wrong where no evidence decides the answer. The Jev review pointed to decider's training trick: an abstain option in some training questions, sometimes with the right answer removed. On 2026-09-27 the row was re-scoped to the then-best reader, decider-4B one-slot with shot-label loss and database episodes (row 80's recipe).

What we tried

With probability 0.1 a training question gets a last option "not listed here"; in a quarter of those the true category is removed from the header and options (the shots keep it), so that option is right. A second setting doubled both rates. Test items (real6_v1_abst): every REAL-6 fold-0 item with the option added, and again with its true category hidden. The planned second arm (uniform targets on evidence-free items) was not run here.

Example prompt

Question: Which of this user's categories does the last transaction belong to?
Options:
(A) Apparel & Eating out & Car
(B) Quorra
  ...
(H) not listed here
Answer: (

The added option; in the "gold hidden" version the right category is missing and (H) is the correct answer. Lettering is illustrative.

How it did

decider-4B% right, real optionsfalse alarms (option chosen, gold present)recall (option chosen, gold hidden)AUROC by option probability
no abstain training88.30.0%61.1%0.963
abstain 0.1, hidden gold 0.2589.31.3%92.3%0.992
abstain 0.2, hidden gold 0.586.60.7%93.3%0.990

What we learned

  • decider already abstains untrained on this task (61% of hidden-gold questions, no false alarms): its pre-training survives our fine-tune.
  • Its own recipe (0.1 / 0.25) finishes the job: 92% caught at 1.3% false alarms, no accuracy cost.
  • A trained option separates missing-category questions better than a confidence threshold (AUROC 0.992 vs 0.969).

Questions it raised

  • This tests the case the trick trains (a hidden category). Abstaining when there is no evidence at all was taken up in row 84.

Row 53 · 2026-09-26 · MODEL-6 · §62 Masked-category encoderdone

A small encoder that scores one marker per category

Why we ran it

The owner pointed out that ModernBERT-style "marker" models had been dismissed on general benchmark rank, which says little about a one-domain categoriser. In row 40 GLiClass could not use the user's example rows; Laya's layout (one scored [MASK] per option) might. A 400M-parameter encoder answering in milliseconds is the production argument at a million users. It uses row 45's groups and row 42's folds.

What we tried

ModernBERT-large (395M), from scratch and from Laya's released checkpoint, with each option's marker scored by a small head and a softmax over options; trained across users on fold 0's other users for 1,500 steps with plain cross-entropy, options shuffled. Scored on REAL-6 and on POI-1 (real places, see row 65), with and without the merchant record, against the 3B.

Example prompt

[CLS] instruction [SEP] [MASK] category 1 [MASK] category 2 ... [SEP] query, optional record, 24 shots as "statement -> category" [SEP]

The input layout: each category's [MASK] gets one score; there is no free text output.

How it did

What we learned

  • The layout does read the shots: on real places, where the name says what the place is, the encoder matches the 3B at about a fortieth of the time per item.
  • On REAL-6 it trails by 15 points without the record and 8 with it; the gap is the decoder's pre-trained knowledge of what merchants are.
  • The record fixes most of that cheaply (+16 to 22), pointing to a production shape: a merchant database plus a small reader.

  • Not yet: all four folds, database episodes for the encoder.

Row 56 · 2026-09-25 to 2026-09-26 · TRAIN-11 · §52 Training efficiency gainsdone

Putting the training loss on every label in the prompt

Why we ran it

Row 47 showed the no-DB categoriser needs 800 to 1,600 steps. Each step pushed 16 sequences of about 850 tokens through the 3B model, yet the loss fell only on the answer's one to five tokens, so each pass taught one label. The owner asked where the hours went and whether the same accuracy could come cheaper.

What we tried

ALL_LABELS=1: the loss on every shot's label too, each predicted from the categories and the shots before it (about 83 supervised tokens in 832 instead of 3), at 100, 200 and 400 steps; the bf16 base against 4-bit; and weighting the final answer to half the loss. Scored on all 20 users, then on row 42's held-out folds; three seeds of 4-bit vs bf16 on the H100.

Example prompt

Transaction: CHECKCARD 09/07 GOLD'S GYM TUCSON AZ | $45.17 | Sat
Category: Grendo
Transaction: HOME DEPOT*03007 FRESNO CA 07/24 | $206.21 | Sun
Category: Quorra
  ...
Transaction: POS ISTMR GRP 5750 | $246.98 | Wed
Category: Plenk

With the all-label loss, every "Category:" answer in the sequence is a training target, not only the last one.

How it did

recipestepstrain minutes (3090)% right, trained users% right, held-out users
plain, 4-bit20017.757.1-
plain, 4-bit80077.671.770.1
plain, 4-bit1,600146.876.7-

Held out, all-label minus plain 800: +4.4 [0.9, 7.8]; database-only real chains 70.4 vs 87.3. Three seeds on H100: 4-bit 80.5 ± 1.0, bf16 80.0 ± 1.4.

What we learned

  • All-label training gives in 100 steps (9 minutes) what 1,600 plain steps (147 minutes) gave, at the same tokens per second and general ability.
  • On new users it is still 4.4 points better than the 800-step recipe at a quarter of the time, but it leans less on what the model knew about real chains (-17), and weighting the answer does not bring that back.
  • bf16 and 4-bit bases give the same categoriser; the owner made bf16 the default.

Questions it raised

  • If the model stops consulting its merchant knowledge, can the fact database fill the gap as training decisions? (row 57)
  • Not done: the all-label loss for the record arm, packing.

Row 57 · 2026-09-26 · REAL-15 · §53 Fact DB as decisionsdone

Teaching the fact database as decisions, not as prose

Why we ran it

Records trained into the weights as sentences reached database-only merchants at 59 to 69 at best. Row 56 showed that supervising the decisions the task makes trains it far faster. The owner suggested (2026-09-25) that loss on more tokens might also be a better way to impart a database. The idea: turn each record into categorisation decisions, scored on row 42's held-out users so memorising one user's answers cannot count.

What we tried

"Database episodes": in half the training episodes, 8 of the 24 shots and the target are replaced by synthetic statement rows of database merchants (all 240, the 60 DB-only ones included), each labelled with that user's own name for the merchant's database category. Against: prose records that state the category, both together, and no DB. All-label loss, 200 steps, H100, four held-out folds, no record in the prompt at test.

Example prompt

Prose arm training text:
Istmere Group is a Travel store that sells airport lounge passes, travel insurance and cruise cabins.

At test (no record):
Transaction: POS ISTMR GRP 5750 | $246.98 | Wed
Category:

The prose arm trains on category-bearing sentences; the episode arm sees the same fact only as labelled statement rows; at test neither gets a note.

How it did

no DB73.470.344.367.611.8

What we learned

  • Taught as decisions, the database puts merchants nobody labelled at 94 on new users with nothing in the prompt (opaque names 12 to 96); the same facts as prose reach 65. It holds in every fold.
  • Every item gains (+16.5 overall), because each episode is also practice at mapping a category into a user's scheme.
  • Knowledge a model must use to decide is best taught in the decision's own format.

Row 59 · 2026-09-26 · REAL-17 · §58 Database episode capacitydone

How many merchants can the weights hold?

Why we ran it

Row 58 found database episodes as good as retrieval at 240 merchants. A production database has millions, and an earlier species experiment saw interference at 5,000 entities, so capacity decides between the two routes.

What we tried

The database padded with generated opaque merchants to 1,000, 5,000 and 20,000. A CPU check before launch showed more steps alone would just repeat the same rows, so database episodes became their own pool entries. Two modes: fixed exposure (30 training rows per merchant, so steps grow with the database) and fixed budget (800 database episodes at every size). H100, bf16, all-label, four held-out folds; REAL-6's 60 DB-only merchants are the measure.

How it did

What we learned

  • No interference up to 20,000 merchants for a rank-64 adapter on a 3B model, given about 30 rows each; nothing else moves (all items 85.8 to 86.7).
  • Spread a fixed budget thinner and the gain fades, to nothing at 0.4 rows per merchant. Exposure, not capacity, is the constraint, and cost grows linearly (20,000 merchants: 89 H100 minutes, about $6).

Questions it raised

  • The minimum rows per merchant (between 7 and 30); beyond 20,000 (a million merchants is about 75 H100-hours at this rate); real merchants instead of generated ones (row 66, on Overture places).

Row 60 · 2026-09-26 · TRAIN-12 · §56 Batch size on H100done

Batch size on the H100

Why we ran it

The owner asked whether batch sizes had been tested on the H100. Row 34 had only compared four passes of four against one pass of 16 at the same 16 sequences per step, and row 56 supplied three seeds of the batch-16 recipe as the reference.

What we tried

EFF_BATCH of 32 and 64 sequences per step against 16: at equal samples (fewer steps for bigger batches; learning rate 1e-4 and scaled by the square root of the batch ratio) and at equal steps (two and four times the data); and passes of 8, 16 and 32 sequences for throughput. All-label no-DB categoriser, bf16, all 20 users, one seed per setting.

How it did

What we learned

  • 16 sequences per step at 1e-4 for 200 steps (three minutes) is already the efficient point.
  • Bigger batches match it only with the rate scaled; batch 64 unscaled stops at 72.3. Two to four times the data per step buys at most about a point for two to four times the compute.

Questions it raised

  • If a larger batch is ever needed, scale the rate by the square root of the batch ratio and lengthen the warmup with it.
