Row 33 · 2026-09-19 · REAL-5 · §38 Production categoriser on REAL-6done

The first trained categoriser, with and without a merchant fact database

Why we ran it

The owner's goal (2026-09-17) is a categoriser that files a user's transactions the way that user would, on their own category names, including merchants the user never labelled but a fact database knows. Row 35 built the REAL-6 test set and found the best untrained reading at 58.0 (the instruct model with the merchant's record in the prompt). This row trains the categoriser and asks whether the fact database helps.

What we tried

Two trained routes: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with a LoRA adapter trained on the exact REAL-6 prompt to output the user's label (label SFT: 3,000 examples, 200 steps, one adapter for all 20 users), and a bge-base embedding model tuned contrastively. The database entered three ways: not at all; "parametric" (each record and three paraphrases trained in as text, at about 1 and then 3.3 passes); or "record in the prompt" (looked up by the merchant's name, an oracle). A first attempt showed the model learning a merchant from other users' labels, so 60 merchants ("DB-only") were held out of all training to measure the database alone.

Example prompt

Note: Istmere Group is a store that sells airport lounge passes, travel insurance and cruise cabins.
Transaction: POS ISTMR GRP 5750 | $246.98 | Wed
Category:

The record-in-prompt arm: the "Note" line is the fact-DB record; the model must map "travel" products to this user's coined word "Plenk".

What we learned

  • Label SFT teaches the format and copying: with the merchant among the 24 shots it copies the label 96.2% of the time.
  • Training across users carries a merchant's category from one user to another: the tuned encoder reads merchants other users labelled at 80.1, as well as the user's own.
  • The record in the prompt reaches merchants nobody labelled at 97.6, coined category names included (95.5). Records trained into the weights did nothing at one pass and +21 points at 3.3 passes, at a 9-point ARC cost.

Moving GPU work to cloud H100s on Modal

Why we ran it

The local RTX 3090 was the bottleneck: the queue held about 35 GPU-hours, the multi-hour species-scale runs left over from row 18 (5,000 species, 20,000 steps) did not fit comfortably, and row 31's sampling took hours. The row waited from 2026-09-19 until the owner set up a Modal workspace on 2026-09-25.

What we tried

scripts/modal_app.py builds the project's own environment from its lock file and runs the repository's scripts unchanged on one H100 (80 GB), saving every output file to a cloud volume; job lists run up to eight containers in parallel. As the check, the same training run (row 56's all-label categoriser, 200 steps, seed 0) was repeated on the 3090 and on the H100, once with the 3090's four passes of four sequences per step and once with one pass of 16 (MICRO=16).

Row 35 · 2026-09-19 · REAL-6 · §37 REAL-6 baseline scoresdone

REAL-6: a test set shaped like real users' budgets

Why we ran it

The owner's goals (2026-09-17) needed a yardstick no earlier set had: users with their own category names, labelled histories, and merchants they have and have not labelled. It reuses the noisy merchant history of row 22 and the record retrieval idea of row 14, and it came before the categoriser (row 33) so that row had something to beat.

What we tried

20 synthetic users, each with 8 to 20 categories made from 12 standard ones by merging, splitting and renaming; each category name is standard ("Groceries"), renamed ("Food shopping") or a coined word ("Zorbit"). Each user has a 300-row labelled history (bank string, amount, weekday). 1,179 test items in cells: merchant seen or unseen in the history, crossed with the name type. Every merchant has a one-sentence fact-DB record. Scored untrained: Qwen2.5-3B base and Instruct with 24 shots of the user's history (with and without the record), and embedding "prototypes" (average embedding per category).

Category: Quorra
  ... 22 more labelled rows ...

Transaction: POS ISTMR GRP 5750 | $246.98 | Wed
Category:

The model scores each of the user's eight category names as the continuation; "Quorra", "Plenk" and "Grendo" are coined names only the shots define.

What we learned

  • The record in the prompt is the biggest single lift; even merchants with meaningless names that the user never labelled reach 52.0 with it (Instruct).

  • Coined category names are every route's weakest cell (under 40 untrained).

Row 36 · 2026-09-19 · DATA-4, DATA-2 · §35 Merchant realism fixesdone

Messy bank strings and products that do not name their category

Why we ran it

An early merchant experiment had left a gap: an embedding model recognised a trained merchant from its bank-statement string 70.8% of the time, and a 0.5B language model was at chance on that string even with the fact in the prompt. A reviewer asked why the obvious fixes (noisy training strings, a normaliser) were never tried (DATA-4), and how much of the category result was recalling products versus bridging from products to a category (DATA-2). The row was split out of row 21 on 2026-09-18; its Needs list row 2.

What we tried

Two routes with the original recipes: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 trained contrastively (96 merchants trained, 24 held out) and Qwen2.5-0.5B fully fine-tuned. Additions: six noisy card-statement renderings per merchant in training (processor prefixes, truncations, vowel-dropped abbreviations, store numbers); a regex normaliser at test; a harder test string with the name cut to eight characters; and a "v2" merchant set where product lists overlap between categories and 20% of merchants sell across categories.

Example prompt

Transaction: POS DEBIT STARBUCKS #0412 SEATTLE WA 03/14
Spending category: Restaurants

Transaction: CARD PURCHASE SHELL OIL 57442 HOUSTON TX
Spending category: Gas & Auto

Transaction: BEST BUY 00021 MINNEAPOLIS MN 11/02
Spending category: Electronics

Transaction: CHKCARD FALVARRO 1032 OMAHA NE
Spending category:

The 0.5B model's truncated-string item; the normaliser would strip the prefix, store number and city, leaving the name stem.

How it did

measure (% right, 12 categories)v1 cleanv1 + renderingsv2 clean
embedding: bank string, trained merchant70.810081.2
embedding: truncated string, raw44.861.542.7
embedding: truncated string, normalised92.791.795.8

What we learned

  • Normalise the statement string first: about a hundred lines of regex take the 0.5B model from chance to its clean-name level, and carry the embedding model on truncated names (44.8 to 92.7).
  • Noisy renderings fix the embedding route but teach the small decoder almost nothing, and cost it backward recall (42.5 to 30.0).
  • Category errors are mostly the products-to-category bridge, not recall: overlapping product lists cost 13 points of category with recall unchanged; multi-category merchants read at half the rate.

Questions it raised

  • Does product ambiguity hurt the trained categoriser on users' own schemes? Row 37 built an ambiguous fact DB this way.
  • Not run: renderings for the 3B decoder, a learned normaliser.

Row 37 · 2026-09-21 · REAL-7 · §43 Record-in-prompt robustnessdone

Stress-testing the record-in-prompt result: ambiguous records, a real retriever, three seeds

Why we ran it

Row 33's headline (90.2 overall, 97.6 on merchants only the database knows) rested on three conveniences: product lists that name their category, a record found by the merchant's true name (an oracle), and one training seed. The owner asked what else the 3090 could do before the cloud move; taking that number apart was the answer.

What we tried

(1) An ambiguous fact DB built as in row 36: 133 of 240 records carry a product from another category, 48 merchants sell across categories; the categoriser retrained on it. (2) A MiniLM retriever tuned only on templated renderings of the database's own names (no user labels), finding the record from the raw statement string, at 240 records and with 5,000 decoy merchants added; its top-1 record replaced the oracle in the prompt. (3) Seeds 1 and 2 of the three SFT arms.

Example prompt

disjoint DB:  Note: Istmere Group is a store that sells airport lounge passes, travel insurance and cruise cabins.
ambiguous DB: Note: Istmere Group is a store that sells airport lounge passes, rental cars and ramen bowls.
Transaction: POS ISTMR GRP 5750 | $246.98 | Wed
Category:

The same item's record in the two databases; the ambiguous one mixes in a restaurant product.

How it did

retriever recall@1 from raw string, 240 records (zero-shot / tuned)79.1 / 99.4

What we learned

  • Ambiguous records cost the retrained categoriser 3 points overall and 9 on database-only merchants; a model trained on clean records and handed ambiguous ones loses twice that.
  • The oracle costs nothing to remove: a cheaply tuned retriever finds 99.4% of records and end-to-end accuracy is unchanged. At production size the hard part is truncated names with near-twins.
  • Over seeds the prompt result stands, and row 33's parametric gain dissolves: its +21 was the best of three seeds.

Questions it raised

  • Does more exposure make records in the weights reliable? (row 39, at three seeds)
  • Not run: the categoriser fed the decoy-index retrieval (estimated about 6 points lower), a retriever trained with the twins as hard negatives.

Row 38 · 2026-09-21 · INFRA-2 · §44 Unsloth-free trainer bugdone

A trainer without unsloth, and the hidden 4-bit default it exposed

Why we ran it

The owner asked whether the unsloth library was more trouble than it was worth after its memory and fused-loss failures in row 31 and row 33. The concrete task: a second implementation of row 33's categoriser trainer with plain transformers + peft, checked against the first.

What we tried

TRAINER=hf trains the same rank-64 LoRA with the same schedule, batches and seed; SCORER=hf scores with the same rule. Then each adapter was read through both loaders, and the earlier results affected by what turned up were re-read on the full-precision (bf16) base.

How it did

measureunslothtransformers + peft
no-DB categoriser, REAL-6 % right57.159.5
record-in-prompt categoriser, % right90.288.5
training minutes (record arm)18.227.6
peak memory, GiB9.112.6

A bf16-trained adapter read on the 4-bit base changed 19.3% of its predictions.

What we learned

  • The two trainers agree within seed spread; the plain path costs 1.5 times the minutes and 3.5 GiB more. Production can use either.
  • unsloth's loader silently loads the 4-bit (NF4) base unless told otherwise. Six scripts written since 2026-09-18 relied on the default. For the categoriser rows that only relabels them (everything was 4-bit QLoRA consistently); for four earlier species re-reads it was a train/score mismatch worth 0 to 9 points, changing no conclusion (one probe's linearity claim got 9 points stronger).
  • New rule in CLAUDE.md: always set load_in_4bit explicitly and score an adapter on the precision it was trained on.

Questions it raised

  • Is the 4-bit base costing accuracy? Row 56 found bf16 and 4-bit the same for this task, and bf16 became the default.
  • Not run: unsloth at bf16, seeds of the new trainer, row 31's teacher at bf16.

Row 40 · 2026-09-22 · BASE-6 · §46 Per-user classifier comparisondone

Off-the-shelf few-shot classifiers: FastFit, GLiClass and a logistic head

Why we ran it

The owner asked about IBM's FastFit, a few-shot classifier built for many similar classes that also scores a query against the label's words, which REAL-6's per-user schemes resemble. A literature scan added GLiClass, a zero-shot label-scoring encoder, and the cheapest baseline, a logistic regression on frozen embeddings. All were compared against row 33's categorisers.

What we tried

One small classifier per user, trained on the 24 prompt shots or the full 300-row history: FastFit on bge-base and mpnet, logistic regression on frozen bge, and GLiClass untrained and fine-tuned across users. Each plain and with the merchant's record appended to the statement in training and at test. Three library bugs had to be repaired first (GLiClass 0.1.20 silently skipped every training step).

Example prompt

Istmr Grp Istmere Group is a store that sells airport lounge passes, travel insurance and cruise cabins.

The encoder's input with the record: the normalised statement string followed by the record; the user's category names are the classes.

How it did

What we learned

  • Without a record no per-user classifier comes near the cross-user models: other users' labels are worth more than any per-user method.

  • Not run: FastFit on the ambiguous DB, one FastFit shared across users, seeds.

Row 41 · 2026-09-22 · REAL-9 · §47 Retrieved shots and copyingdone

Choosing which past transactions go in the prompt

Why we ran it

The owner framed categorisation as a recommender problem: the user's history is the interaction log, and picking the prompt's 24 example rows is the retrieval step. Every prompt so far used one fixed random block per user. Pinterest's published rules (TransAct V2, Pinner Progression) suggested better ones, and row 37's retriever made similarity search possible on row 33's categorisers.

What we tried

Four shot rules per query: "recent" (here a random block, since REAL-6 has no time), "nearest" (the 24 most similar rows), "transact" (8 recent plus the nearest row of each category) and "cluster" (spread over clusters of the user's rows). Applied at test only to the untrained model and the fixed-shot adapters, and to adapters trained with the rule. 28 steps, 12.7 GPU hours. Midway, an audit (row 45) supplied corrected groups for reading the results.

Example prompt

Transaction: CHECKCARD 09/07 GOLD'S GYM TUCSON AZ | $45.17 | Sat
Category: Grendo
  ... other shots ...
Transaction: CARD PURCHASE GOLD'S GYM 9253 TUCSON AZ | $140.41 | Fri
Category:

When a shot is the same merchant as the query, the model can simply copy its label; similarity rules make this happen far more often.

How it did

arm and rule (% right)merchant in historydecided by its categoryall
nearest-row lookup, no model98.619.147.5
SFT no DB, fixed shots65.359.357.1
SFT no DB, nearest shots at test99.158.570.1
SFT no DB, trained with nearest98.636.157.5
SFT + record, fixed shots94.197.490.2
SFT + record, transact at test98.697.493.0
SFT + record, trained with nearest99.179.283.5

What we learned

  • Every rule's gain (up to 25 points) is the user's own label for the same merchant, which a lookup gives at 98.6 without any model. On items decided by the merchant's category, no rule moves a trained model at test.
  • TransAct's block is the best at test (93.0 vs 90.2, paired +2.8) because it keeps every category among the shots; a pure similarity block leaves the right label out 31% of the time.
  • Training with retrieved shots teaches copying (final loss near 0.001) and costs 20 to 30 points on what cannot be copied, including database-only merchants (97.6 to 76).

Questions it raised

  • Train with retrieved shots but withhold the query's own merchant (row 46).
  • REAL-6 users never relabel or file a merchant two ways, so whether shots beat a lookup when they disagree needs a new set (row 43).

Row 42 · 2026-09-26 · REAL-10 · §51 Held-out user generalisationdone

Users the model has never seen, and rename augmentation

Why we ran it

Every REAL-6 number so far scored the same 20 users whose histories trained the adapter (row 33). Production meets over a million users whose schemes it never saw. The audit (row 45) turned the planned single split into four folds, and row 47 set the no-DB arm at 800 steps.

What we tried

Users split into four folds of five; each fold's adapter trains on the other fifteen and scores its five, so all 1,179 items are read by a model that never saw that user. No-DB arm at 800 steps, record-in-prompt arm at 200. Then "rename augmentation": in each training episode, each category name is replaced by a fresh coined word with probability 0.5, consistently in the list, shots and answer, so memorising a user's vocabulary stops paying. The no-DB augmentation was measured on row 56's newer all-label recipe.

Example prompt

Categories: Apparel & Eating out & Car, Gadgets & Food shopping, Quorra, Pharmacy & Health, Entertainment, Plenk, Animals, Grendo

Transaction: CHECKCARD 09/07 GOLD'S GYM TUCSON AZ | $45.17 | Sat
Category: Grendo

For a held-out user, only the shots say that "Grendo" means gyms; the model cannot have memorised it.

How it did

arm (% right first)all, trained usersall, held outcoined names, trainedcoined names, held out
SFT no DB, 800 steps71.770.173.963.9
SFT + record in prompt90.281.691.666.7
no DB all-label, held out: without / with rename-74.5 / 77.6-73.1 / 85.1

What we learned

  • Standard names transfer fully to a new user (record arm 99.6 vs 99.8).
  • Coined names fall 10 points (no DB) and 25 (record): the models had been memorising the training users' private vocabularies rather than reading them off the shots.
  • Rename augmentation recovers most of that (+12 and +14 on coined names) at no cost on standard names. From here every categoriser trains with it and is scored on held-out users; the new-user record arm reads 86.8.

Questions it raised

  • Other augmentation rates; more than 20 users (row 43).
  • The held-out folds became the test bed for row 57's database episodes, which were not yet combined with rename augmentation (later row 81).

Row 43 · added 2026-09-21, amended 2026-09-22 · REAL-11 · first version in §93 A more realistic synthetic populationdoing

A successor test set shaped like the real population

Why we ran it

The owner described the real population on 2026-09-21: over a million users, most on the default categories, some with custom labels, filing merchants for personal reasons, with recent inputs mattering most. REAL-6 (built for row 33) cannot test that. Row 41 showed REAL-6 users never relabel, and the audit of row 45 found its items are decided by a lookup or by the merchant's standard category, so the set had to be rebuilt so neither decides it.

What we tried

Planned: mostly default-scheme users and some custom; the same merchant filed differently by different users; a time axis with next-transaction prediction, relabelling and new-category events, the latest label winning; per-shot provenance (typed, accepted, corrected) and elapsed time; a uniformly drawn slice for replaying shot policies; short histories (0, 5, 25 rows); 200 or more users for held-out folds; a statement-rendering family unseen by the normaliser and retriever; overlapping amounts; the lookup and lookup-then-model hybrid as standard columns. A first version (real7_v1, 200 new users) was built and scored zero-shot on 2026-09-27 under row 93: REAL-6-trained readers reached 82 against an 84.8 ceiling (untrained 51). PLAN.md still marks this row as in progress.

What we learned

  • A set where neither a merchant lookup nor the standard category gives the answer is needed before shot selection, relabelling or collaborative signals can be judged.

Questions it raised

Was the no-database categoriser simply under-trained?

Why we ran it

Row 39's records-in-the-weights arms trained for 800 and 1,600 steps against the no-DB arm's 200, and row 45 showed most of their gain was on merchants users had labelled, which the records cannot explain. If the no-DB arm gains the same with more steps, every categoriser so far used a quarter of the training it needed.

What we tried

The no-DB SFT categoriser unchanged except for length: 400 steps (one seed), 800 (three seeds) and 1,600 (one seed), read in row 45's groups and on ARC-Easy and MMLU. 9.2 GPU hours.
