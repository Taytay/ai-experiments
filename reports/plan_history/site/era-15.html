Row 191 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27 · proposedtodo

Counting split transactions on the owner's budget

Why we ran it

A split transaction is one purchase divided across several categories, like a big-box receipt that is part groceries and part household goods. Our real-budget scorer threw every split away: 297 of 23,841 approved transactions (about 1.2%) and their 674 parts. Those parts are neither scored nor shown to the model as history. The owner said "Yes" to scoring them, after the v4 reads in row 190.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. First, score each part of a split as its own item: the parent's payee, date and account, the part's amount, and the part's category as the answer, reported as a separate "split line" group. Second, show past parts as history rows marked as part of a split, so a payee the owner usually splits still teaches its categories. Then check whether that changes accuracy on the existing items, in paired reads over two or more seeds. A later idea is a flag that says "this one should probably be split". Everything stays private, aggregates only.

Example prompt

Payee: Northgate Warehouse Club | $146.20 | Sun
Your earlier rows:
  Northgate Warehouse Club | $88.00 (part of a split) | Groceries
  Northgate Warehouse Club | $34.50 (part of a split) | Household Goods
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: two parts of one earlier split, shown as history rows.

Questions it raised

  • How often do split parts get the right category, compared with whole transactions?
  • Does showing split history help payees the owner usually splits, or confuse the model on whole purchases from the same store?

Row 192 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §176 Behavioural rows plus crowd linedone

Do behaviour-chosen look-alikes and the crowd line add up?

Why we ran it

Row 189 showed that picking look-alike payees by other people's behaviour helps first-time payees. Row 190 sized the crowd line on clean data. The two signals answer different questions. Behavioural rows say "which of my payees are like this one". The crowd line says "where do people put this one". The owner said "Do 1 and 2": repeat row 189 on the v4 world, and try it together with the line.

What we tried

We rebuilt the 64 purpose clusters on the v4 world. We made held-out test sets in six versions: similar rows from the household's own rows only, by spelling (character n-grams, short overlapping letter runs), or by behaviour, each with and without the crowd line. The G4 and GCD4 models from row 190 read every version, two seeds each, with no retraining.

Example prompt

Payee: SQ *BLUE KETTLE CAFE | $6.85 | Tue
Others filed this payee as: Coffee (22), Eating out (9)
Similar earlier payees:
  Morning Ritual Coffee | $5.40 | Coffee Shops
  Bean & Barrel         | $7.25 | Coffee Shops
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: both signals in one prompt.

What we learned

  • The two cross-user signals stack: GCD4's first-time payees rise from 55.7 to 67.9, +12.2 over own rows alone.
  • Behavioural rows add +2.3 over spelling rows without the line and +0.8 with it. The line adds +3.1 to +4.6 over the rows alone.

  • The owner's budget has no crowd data, so these gains cannot show there yet.

Row 193 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-28 · §176 Behavioural rows plus crowd linedone

One model with merchant knowledge, the crowd line and behavioural look-alikes

Why we ran it

Earlier rows built three separate improvements: training episodes that teach what 20,000 merchants are, the crowd line (row 190), and behaviour-chosen similar rows (row 192). Under the owner's "Do 1 and 2", this row asked whether one model trained on all of them beats the models that carry one at a time.

What we tried

We trained three new arms on the v4 world, two seeds each. K4 adds merchant-knowledge episodes to 40% of training. CDB4 trains with the crowd line (dropped from 40% of items) and with behavioural similar rows. KCDB4 has both. We compared them with G4 and GCD4 from row 190 on the same synthetic sets as row 192. We also read the owner's budget, which has no crowd line and uses spelling-chosen rows, and recorded first guess, top 3 and top 5.

Row 194 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §177 History-aware encodersdone

Small encoders that compare a purchase with the household's own past purchases

Why we ran it

Row 187's two-tower encoder matched a transaction against a description of each category and got only 47.2% right on the owner's budget. The owner suggested to "embed the historical similar transactions", and to "try all 3 approaches ... on its own, or to enhance an LLM prediction".

What we tried

We fine-tuned a small open text encoder (bge-small) so that a transaction sits close to earlier transactions filed in the same category. Training used InfoNCE: the model is shown a transaction, one right partner and many wrong ones, and is rewarded for picking the right one. The right partner was an earlier transaction in the same category, half the time from another payee. The hard wrong one was the most similar-looking earlier transaction filed somewhere else. We read the encoder three ways. (1) A kNN vote: the 20 nearest earlier transactions vote for their categories. (2) Prototypes: each category becomes the average of what was filed in it. (3) The query also carries its 5 nearest earlier transactions and where they went. Each was used alone and fused with decider: decider's score plus a weight W times the encoder's.

Example prompt

ANCHOR:   Maple Grocers | $57.09 | Fri
POSITIVE: Brookshire's | $83.40 | Sat   (filed: Groceries)
NEGATIVE: Maple Grocers | $141.02 | Sun (filed: Party Supplies)

Illustrative, invented: one training triplet. The negative has the same payee but went elsewhere, so the encoder must learn more than spelling.

What we learned

  • History is what the encoder was missing: comparing with earlier transactions lifts the owner's budget from 47 to 68 with no LLM.
  • Prototypes are weak (48). An average blurs categories that hold many kinds of thing, such as trips or a person.
  • Used as a gate, kNN can settle about a third of transactions without the LLM at no loss.

Questions it raised

  • Would late interaction or Pinterest's TransAct-style history do better? (row 195)

Row 195 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §177 History-aware encodersdone

Pinterest-style history and late interaction for the encoders

Why we ran it

After row 194 the owner asked: "Is it possible to also try the late interaction models? ... Especially the Pinterest stuff." Pinterest's TransAct V2 got its biggest gains by changing which past actions the ranker sees: the most recent few plus the nearest few, chosen per candidate.

What we tried

We built three more readers. (4) TransAct: the query carries the 5 most recent and the 5 nearest earlier filings, with where each went and how long ago (elapsed time comes from Zepto's system). (5) MaxSim: a category is the set of its filings, not one average. Its score is the mean of the query's 3 best matches among them. This is late interaction: compare many small pieces, keep the best matches. (6) ColBERT: late interaction one level down, at the word-piece level, used to re-rank encoder 1's 50 nearest. Each was read alone and fused with decider.

Example prompt

Query: Maple Grocers | $57.09 | Fri
Recent:  Fern St Pharmacy | $14.20 | Health (1 day ago)
         Maple Grocers | $61.30 | Groceries (2 days ago)
         ...
Nearest: Maple Grocers | $49.88 | Groceries (9 days ago)
         Maple Grocers | $141.02 | Party Supplies (40 days ago)
         ...

Illustrative, invented: a TransAct-style query, 5 recent plus 5 nearest, each with its category and age.

What we learned

  • MaxSim is the best single reader: 69.6 first, and the right category in its top 10 93.3% of the time, above decider's ~90.
  • Fused with decider it adds +1.0 / +1.5 overall and +1.6 / +2.1 on first-time payees.
  • Word-piece ColBERT adds nothing over whole-transaction vectors, but it gates well: it can file half the transactions alone at no loss.
  • MaxSim's scores are too flat to gate, because its temperature is not calibrated.

Questions it raised

Row 196 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

A running summary of each household that shifts where payees go

Why we ran it

The history encoders in row 194 and row 195 see past filings one at a time. The owner, thinking like a recommender system, wanted "incorporating recent knowledge ... to perturb the mappings". On a trip, the same coffee shop may belong under the trip's category. After the owner reorganises categories, old filings point the wrong way. Pinterest recomputes a user's embedding on every request for the same reason: people "change their status instantly".

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. A small user tower (a network that turns the household's last N filings into one state vector) reads each filing's encoder vector, its age and its category. The state is added to the query, and training rewards the model when the same payee maps differently inside a trip window or after a reorganisation. A second arm borrows PinnerFormer's objective: train the state to predict the categories of the next two weeks. Zepto's gate between long-term and recent history is the model for mixing the two. It would be read like row 194, alone and fused with decider.

Questions it raised

  • Can a state vector learn trips and phases from synthetic households, where routing is mostly by time?
  • Does it help the owner's catch-all and person categories, where prototypes failed?

Row 197 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

Standard codes for categories, so a model can name a purpose before it knows the household

Why we ran it

Every household names its categories differently. Row 189's 64 purpose clusters already group "Dining out" with "Eating out" across households. Pinterest's UniPinRec (2026) and Google's TIGER give each item a short "semantic ID", a code built from nested clusters, and have a model generate the code directly.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. Build standard category codes by residual k-means: cluster the category vectors, then cluster what is left over inside each cluster, and so on, giving a code of a few levels. Row 189's clusters would be level 1. Decider, or a smaller model, generates a transaction's code, and the household's category carrying that code wins. This could serve new users and first-time payees, where there is no history to match. It would also give the crowd line a shared vocabulary.

Example prompt

Payee: SQ *HARBOR NOODLE 4471 | $23.40 | Sat
Code: 17-04-2      (17 = eating out, 04 = sit-down, 2 = weekend)
This household's category with code 17-04: Date Night

Illustrative, invented: the codes and their meanings are made up to show the idea.

Questions it raised

  • Do codes stay stable when categories mean different things to different people (trips, people, catch-alls)?
  • Is a generated code more accurate than ranking the household's own category names?

Row 198 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

Describing a merchant by its neighbours, not just its name

Why we ran it

The history encoders from row 194 are weakest on first-time payees, where the household's own history is thin (about 50 right first, against 76 to 77 for payees filed before). Pinterest's PinSage, and its successor OmniSage, build an item's vector from its own content plus its neighbours in a graph. Row 189 already has such a graph: bank strings linked to the purpose clusters other households file them under.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. A bank string's vector would combine its own text with its neighbours' vectors over the merchant-and-category graph, weighted by other households' filings and row 189's cluster counts. It would be trained with row 187's two-tower objective, which pulls matching payees and categories together and pushes others apart. The aim is that a never-seen string inherits what the crowd knows about similar strings.

Questions it raised

  • Does a graph-informed vector help first-time payees beyond behavioural similar rows (row 192)?
  • What does it do for strings that few or no other households have filed?

Row 199 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §178 Fair fusion and amountsdone

Choosing how to combine the encoders with decider on synthetic data first

Why we ran it

§177 History-aware encoders (row 194, row 195) found that adding an encoder's opinion to decider's lifted the owner's budget by 1.0 to 1.5 points. But the weight W was picked from four values on the owner's budget itself, so some of the gain could be selection. A fair test chooses every setting somewhere else, then applies it once. The owner said: "Proceed with the plan."

What we tried

For each reader (kNN, TransAct, MaxSim) we choose three settings on 100 held-out synthetic v4 households, using decider G4's reads from two seeds. The first is the fusion weight W. The second is MaxSim's temperature T, which controls how sharp its scores are; row 195 found them too flat to gate. The third is a gate G: the encoder files alone wherever its top probability is at least G, and the fused system decides elsewhere. G is chosen as the largest share of transactions the encoder can take within 0.1 points of fused accuracy. These settings are then applied unchanged to the owner's budget, aggregates only.

  • The encoders' clear value is cost: a gate files a fifth of transactions without the 4B model at no loss.
  • Whether any fusion can pay depends on how different the two readers' errors are, measured next in row 201.

  • How much of the owner's budget can a calibrated gate hand to the encoder with no loss?

Row 200 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §178 Fair fusion and amountsdone

Treating amounts as numbers, not words

Why we ran it

The encoders read "$9.99" as text. Text pieces do not carry size: "$9.99" and "$10.00" share almost no pieces, and an exact repeat (a monthly bill) is not marked as special. The owner asked: "is representing them as strings a bad idea? ... embed 'less than $10', '$10-25' ...", and gave the case to watch: "A small amount of money at QT is likely to be a treat, not gas." This follows the fusion settings fixed in row 199.

What we tried

The arms, on encoder 1 and MaxSim: A, amount as text (today); B, no amount at all (retrained); C, B plus a log-amount kernel, so a neighbour counts more when its amount is close on a log scale (width chosen on synthetic data); D, a bonus for an exact amount match. Two arms run only if C or D shows that amounts matter: E, bucket words such as "under $10", and F, a learned numeric input. They are judged on the owner's budget with settings fixed on synthetic data, and payees the owner has already filed under two or more categories (the QT case) get their own line. The synthetic households barely route by amount; their one rule is weekday coffee under $12. A v5 generator with amount-dependent routing (gas-station snacks against fuel, small against large online orders) would let training see it.

Example prompt

QT 0412 | $4.12  | Wed   -> Treats
QT 0412 | $41.80 | Wed   -> Fuel

Arm C: neighbour weight x exp(-(log 4.12 - log 41.80)^2 / width)

Illustrative, invented amounts: the same payee at two sizes should go to two categories. Arm C makes a $41.80 neighbour count for little when the query is $4.12.

How it did

What we learned

  • Amounts carry real signal: without them MaxSim loses 7.3 overall and 20.6 on payees filed in more than one category (the QT case).
  • A numeric closeness kernel restores almost all of it but adds nothing on top of the text; an exact-amount bonus adds nothing. Bucket words and a learned numeric input were not built.
  • Synthetic households barely route by amount, so they cannot tune amount settings; a generator with amount-dependent routing would.

Questions it raised

  • Does dropping the amount text hurt at all, or is the amount mostly noise to the encoder today?
  • Is a v5 synthetic generator with amount-dependent routing needed before training can learn this?
