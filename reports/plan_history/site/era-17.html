Row 209 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §199 Larger encoder basesdone

Do bigger encoders help?

Why we ran it

The encoders so far started from a 33-million-parameter model. The owner asked "Is there a modernBERT base for this that has been trained on a lot of tokens?" and added "I'm impressed that 33M parameters can do so well. That makes me hopeful for much bigger models!" The papers suggested the training objective matters more than size on short text, so any gain should show up on first-time payees, where knowing the world pays.

What we tried

One recipe at each size: the knowledge-and-alias stage from §191 A small modern base: Ettin-encoder-32M (teaching the encoder what kinds of business merchant strings are), then the late-interaction history training. Bases, all openly licensed: Ettin-32M, Ettin-150M, ModernBERT-base (150M) and Ettin-400M. Round 1 trained on v4 synthetic households; round 2 on the v5 households that rank arms more like the owner's budget. One seed each, on Modal.

Example prompt

Knowledge stage pair:
  "POS DEBIT CEDARVIEW TIRE #112 OAKFORD"  <->  "kind: auto repair, tires"
History stage query:
  CEDARVIEW TIRE #112 | $412.60 | Mon

Illustrative, invented: the same two-stage recipe at every model size.

Row 210 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25, MODEL-26 · §185 The late-interaction decision modeldone

A small decision model that scores every category at once

Why we ran it

Jev and Clef-style models make a decision by scoring each option. Late-interaction encoders such as ColBERT compare a query and a document word piece by word piece (MaxSim) rather than as two whole vectors. The owner asked: "Has anyone built a Jev-like model with a late interaction model?" Nothing had been published. Row 195's ColBERT was a natural start.

What we tried

A 33M model (bge-small token vectors) scores every one of the household's categories in one pass, trained with a softmax over those options plus a calibration term (Brier). Arms, one seed each on Modal: a0 one document per category (its name and last 8 payees); a1 a0 plus the transaction's 5 nearest earlier filings in the query; a2 to a4 scoring tweaks from the papers (soft interaction, a pooled-plus-MaxSim hybrid, learned word weights); a5 each of the 50 nearest earlier filings, plus every category name, as its own candidate, with a category's probability summed over its candidates; a6 a5 with the tweaks. All read on 50 held-out households.

Example prompt

Query:   Harbor Noodle | $24.10 | Sat
         near: Pho Lantern | $31.10 -> Dining Out; Golden Wok | $18.75 -> Dining Out
Options: [Dining Out: Pho Lantern, Golden Wok, ...]
         [Groceries: Maple Grocers, ...]
         [Date Night: Rosie's Diner, ...]
-> softmax over options

Illustrative, invented: arm a1's query with its nearest earlier filings, against one document per category.

What we learned

  • What the model sees decides it: a fixed category summary loses 9 points; adding the nearest history (a1) or scoring each earlier filing (a5) recovers them and passes the best plain encoder.
  • How it scores barely matters: the paper tweaks moved accuracy by under a point.
  • Calibration is good throughout and best with per-filing candidates (1.7%), usable for an auto-filing threshold.

Questions it raised

  • Would a bigger base help? (row 209)

Row 211 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25, MODEL-26 · §185 The late-interaction decision modeldone

Adding layers so history and options can talk to each other

Why we ran it

In row 210's model the transaction and each option meet only through a fixed MaxSim. The owner asked: "Could we add some layers to the encoder to allow for more interaction between the history and output tokens?" An LLM sees the options together in its prompt; this model did not.

What we tried

Starting from a1, 3,000 more training steps on the local 3090, with small new layers that start as a no-op: c0 no new layers (the control); x two cross-attention blocks where the transaction's word pieces attend to each option's; xm plus 10 memory tokens, one per nearest earlier filing (its vector plus its category's); xml plus a listwise layer where the options attend to each other before the softmax. Same loss, same 50 held-out households.

Example prompt

Query tokens:   SQ * BLUE KETTLE CAFE $6.85 Tue
Memory tokens:  [Morning Ritual Coffee -> Coffee Shops] x10
Options:        Coffee Shops | Dining Out | Treats | ...
cross-attention: query tokens look at each option's tokens
listwise layer:  options look at each other

Illustrative, invented: where the three kinds of new layer sit.

How it did

Model (50 held-out households)Top-1 / top-3 / top-10First-time payee top-1BrierCalibration error
a1 (row 210)75.0 / 91.2 / 96.962.50.3684.0%
c0 a1 + 3,000 more steps75.7 / 91.4 / 96.963.40.3633.4%
x + cross-attention75.5 / 91.3 / 96.963.00.3642.8%

What we learned

  • The interaction layers add nothing: -0.2 to -0.3 against the control, within one-seed noise. They improve calibration slightly.
  • More updates do help: the control's extra 3,000 steps gave +0.7 overall and +0.9 on first-time payees.
  • The gap to decider is not reasoning this model lacks at this scale.

Row 212 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25, MODEL-28, DATA-7 · §194 Kind lines in the late-interaction modeldone

Telling the small model what kind of business a payee is

Why we ran it

The owner asked to "make sure we have a plan for injecting world knowledge in from our database of places and payees". For decider, a "Kind:" line from a database lifted first-time payees from 60 to 77 on a blind set (§144 Canonical kind lines), but on the owner's budget the lookups were right for only about half the matches and a model trusting them lost 1.5 (§168 Kind lines from a lookup). So the encoder had to learn how far to trust a lookup.

What we tried

Arm (a), the lookup as text: each transaction gets "| kind: ... (match high/mid/low)". In training the lines were deliberately wrong at the rates measured on real lookups (high 77% right, mid 65%, low 47%; 57% of payees covered), so the model learns to discount weak matches. Two seeds. On the owner's budget the real lookups were used, locally and privately. Arm (b), the lookup as memory tokens, was not run. Arms (c) and (e), knowledge and alias pretraining, ran in row 222 (§191 A small modern base: Ettin-encoder-32M); arm (d), distilling decider, ran in row 223 (§192 Distilling decider into the late-interaction model).

Example prompt

CEDARVIEW TIRE #112 OAKFORD | $412.60 | Mon | kind: auto repair, tires (match high)
GRN LEAF 0091               | $18.20  | Thu | kind: florist (match low)

Illustrative, invented: two transactions with kind lines; the second is a weak match the model should not trust much.

How it did

% right first (seed 0 / seed 1)Without kind line (h15)With kind line (kl)
Synthetic, all73.4 / 73.373.7 / 73.6
Synthetic, first-time payees60.5 / 60.261.7 / 61.5
Rational households, bank strings, new merchants57.063.0 / 63.9
Rational households, clean names, new merchants60.365.3
blind_v2 (no kind data)81.881.9

What we learned

  • The encoder uses a lookup and learns to discount it: new real merchants gain 5 to 7 points without hurting payees filed before.
  • The owner's budget gains nothing measurable (+0.15): its first-time payees are people and local businesses that get no line or a weak one.
  • Better lookups (payee resolution, row 160) are the lever, not the model. The kind line stays as a harmless option.

Row 213 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §184 EVoC for the crowd category clustersdone

A newer clustering method for the crowd's purpose groups

Why we ran it

Row 189 grouped every household's categories into 64 purpose clusters with k-means, and row 192 showed behaviour-chosen look-alikes built on them help first-time payees. The owner pointed to "a newer form of fast clustering of embeddings that allows for a fast treemap of related topics": EVoC, which picks its own number of clusters and builds layers from fine to coarse.

What we tried

We clustered the same 42,555 category texts with k-means 64 and with EVoC (every layer, and a 64-cluster setting), on the CPU. (a) We compared each clustering with the generator's own purposes (ARI and NMI measure agreement; purity is the share of a cluster that shares its majority purpose). (b) A cheap proxy before any decider read: for first-time payees in 50 held-out households, does the household's nearest payee by crowd profile share the right category? (c) decider reads only if (a) and (b) showed a gain.

Example prompt

Group: Food; Name: Takeout & restaurants; recently: Harbor Noodle, Golden Wok, Pho Lantern
Group: Fun;  Name: Eating out;            recently: Rosie's Diner, Taco Haven, Pho Lantern
-> same cluster?

Illustrative, invented: two households' category texts that a good clustering should put together.

How it did

ClusteringClustersUnclusteredARIPurityNeighbour hit@1
k-means 64 (row 189)640%0.3000.80945.9
EVoC, finest layer87671.3%0.0420.93444.2
EVoC layer 29025.0%0.4050.82845.8
EVoC coarsest layer829.6%0.7570.59641.7
EVoC, 64 clusters asked6421.0%0.3890.82345.7

Proxy references: spelling neighbours 41.0, the household's most-used category 20.0.

What we learned

  • For the job the clusters do, EVoC equals k-means (within 0.2 on the proxy). The clustering is not what limits behavioural rows.
  • EVoC's better agreement at coarse layers comes from lumping all trips (a quarter of categories) together, while purity falls.
  • It leaves a fifth to a quarter of categories unclustered. Part (c) was not run.
  • It is fast (8 to 10 s on 42k vectors) and its tree may suit a category treemap.

Questions it raised

  • Can clustering suggest a category scheme for a new user? (row 214)

Row 214 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27 · §186 Suggesting a category scheme for a new userdone

Suggesting categories for a brand-new user from their transactions

Why we ran it

The owner asked: "What if I wanted to take a whole list of transactions and have them get auto-clustered for a user, and have categories suggested?" and "I'm curious to run EVoC on synthetic data, just to learn". This was exploratory, on synthetic households only.

What we tried

We took 50 held-out synthetic households and hid their categories. Each distinct payee became a point, weighted by how often it appears, described by its encoder vector, its crowd profile (how other households file it), and amount and rhythm features. We clustered with EVoC and with agglomerative clustering told the true number of categories (an oracle). Two baselines needed no clustering: give each payee its most common crowd cluster (B1), or its most common crowd category name (B2). We scored against the real categories: ARI, the number of merges and splits a user would make, and the unclustered share.

Example prompt

Payees (count):  Maple Grocers (48), Harbor Noodle (12), Splash Swim School (10), ...
B1 suggestion:   Maple Grocers -> cluster "groceries"
                 Harbor Noodle -> cluster "eating out"
                 Splash Swim School -> cluster "kids' activities"

Illustrative, invented: the crowd lookup gives each payee the purpose other households most often use for it.

How it did

Features, method (means over 50 households)ARIClusters (true 49)EditsUnclustered
B1 most common crowd cluster0.751313675.7%
B2 most common crowd name0.513654365.7%
Crowd, agglomerative told k = 49 (oracle)0.581491700
Encoder + crowd, EVoC0.4903129818.5%
Encoder alone, EVoC0.3461939424.3%
Amount and rhythm, EVoC0.134687978.2%

30.9% of payees are filed under 2+ categories and carry 86.7% of transactions, so no payee-level scheme can exceed 76.6% purity.

What we learned

  • Ask the crowd before clustering: a simple lookup (ARI 0.75) beats every clustering of the household's own payees.
  • What payees say helps; how money moves does not: amount and rhythm features halve agreement (a monthly bill and a regular coffee look alike).
  • Personal purposes (whose money, which trip) are invisible at payee level. A suggestion tool should propose the crowd's scheme, then ask about the personal splits.

Questions it raised

  • Could a suggester ask a new user a few questions to find the personal splits?

The small model's query showed 5 nearest filings, undated. On trips the owner expected that "once it starts seeing a trip category used for a given type of payee ... it might learn to use that recent category". After row 216, a test set that predicts real-data rankings was also needed.

What we tried

Teaching the small model to trust the household over the name

Why we ran it

decider's training recipe has tricks the small model never had. One is override episodes: a household's payees moved, every time, to an unexpected category, so the model learns that history beats names. Row 220's rational households showed the small model failing exactly there, and the owner asked "How do we fix the override issue?" and said "we can't average a payee's embedding/category".

What we tried

One seed per arm, 3,000 steps on p2's query, run in parallel on Modal L40S GPUs: o1 override episodes (10% of payees moved); n1 category renames; h1 1,000 training households instead of 200; "all" those three together; pp a learned prior from the payee's earlier filings; ppo and pp2o the prior plus override; md each earlier filing as a candidate beside the category documents, and mdo with override; m0 a5's bare-query design retrained. Time-on-candidate arms (m1 to m3) were also tried. Read on synthetic, blind_v2 and rational households; the finalists once on the owner's budget.

Row 219 · 2026-10-06 · INFRA · §188 The cheapest Modal GPU for the small encoder modelsdone

Which cloud GPU is cheapest for the small models?

Why we ran it

The 33M late-interaction model trained 2 to 3 times faster on the local 3090 than on Modal's H100, which suggested the H100 was wasted on it. The owner asked: "Are there a100s that are just as fast and cheaper on modal?" and then "Yes please!" to a test.

What we tried

One identical 600-step training run (row 217's p2 query, 50 households, same seed) on five Modal GPU types, in one job list where each job names its own GPU. We measured seconds per step, excluding data preparation, and worked out the GPU cost of a full 3,000-step arm.

Example prompt

[
  {"tag": "r219-l40s", "gpu": "L40S", "cmds": ["li_decider.py train STEPS=600 ..."]},
  {"tag": "r219-h100", "gpu": "H100", "cmds": ["li_decider.py train STEPS=600 ..."]}
]

Row 221 · 2026-10-06 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §193 Masked history-row labelsdone

Hiding some of the history's answers and asking the model to fill them in

Why we ran it

The late-interaction model reads a transaction plus the household's earlier filings, and compares it piece by piece with a short document for each category. Each training query taught it one thing: the category of that one transaction. The owner asked: "are we able to use a masked token objective to get it to learn more relationships, by having it fill in missing payee tokens or category tokens? Or are we simply pushing embeddings closer and further away?" The decider LLM had gained from being graded on every answer in its prompt, not only the last one.

What we tried

In training, each history row in a query had its category replaced by a mask with probability HMASK, and the model had to predict the hidden categories as well as the main one. That gives about 76 extra graded answers per training step on top of its 32 main ones, at no extra encoder cost. Arms on the Ettin-32M recipe from row 222 (ekmv): HMASK 0 (control), 0.15 (h15, two seeds) and 0.3 (h30). Nothing is hidden when the model is read. The second half of the question, masked-token pretraining of the encoder itself, ran in row 222.

Example prompt

Query:  SQ *PINE CONE PIZZA | $18.40 | Sat
Recent: CHEVRON 0091 | $44.10 | Trip: Lake Week (1 day ago)
        LAKESIDE MARINA | $25.00 | [MASK] (1 day ago)
        HARBOR BAIT & TACKLE | $12.75 | Trip: Lake Week (2 days ago)
Nearest: SQ *PINE CONE PIZZA | $21.00 | [MASK] (90 days ago)

Illustrative, invented: two history rows have their categories hidden, and the model is graded on guessing them too. Most hidden rows are recent ones, so it has to learn that a run of trip filings means "this is a trip".

Swapping in a small, modern encoder: Ettin-32M

Why we ran it

The late-interaction model had been built on bge-small, an older 33M-parameter encoder that reads at most 512 tokens. Ettin-encoder-32M is the same size but newer (the ModernBERT design), reads 8,000 tokens and ships the head needed for masked-token training. The owner: "try the small ettin model soon so that we have more context with a fast, small model that makes iteration fast ... I just want to learn how fast we can push this small model".

What we tried

First, optional stages that teach the encoder about merchants before it learns households: k, a contrastive stage on 371,866 bank-style merchant strings paired with their kind and 253,574 pairs of two spellings of one merchant; mk, masked-token pretraining on the same strings and then k; kmv, k in the late-interaction form. Then row 218's best recipe (o1: dated history query plus override training) trained from each start, plus a ColBERT-pretrained Ettin (mx) and a version with twice the history (eklong). One seed each, two for ekmv; L40S GPUs on Modal.

Teaching the small model from the big model's answers

Why we ran it

decider-4B knows far more about real merchants than a 32M encoder. Papers on late-interaction retrieval (ColBERT-Zero, LITE) found that learning from a strong teacher's scores, not only from right and wrong answers, gives most of the benefit of much bigger training. Row 222 had just shown that knowledge taught in a separate stage fades during history training.

What we tried

decider scored 30,000 transactions from 300 synthetic households it had never trained on (74.3% right first, 20.6 minutes on an H100). The late-interaction model then trained on those households too, with half of each step's transactions teacher-scored and an extra loss pulling its probabilities over the household's categories towards decider's. kd0 is the same run without that loss (the extra households alone). Arms on bge-small and on Ettin's ekmv start, one seed each.

  Pets 0.71 | Household 0.14 | Gifts 0.06 | ...
Student target: match these probabilities, not just "Pets"

Illustrative, invented: a soft target from the teacher, which also says how plausible the wrong answers are.

Treating each earlier filing as its own candidate, on Ettin

Why we ran it

Row 218's best bge model on the owner's budget, mdo, offered each earlier filing as a separate candidate beside the category documents, but read at 11 ms a transaction against 2.7 for the document reader. The plan was to make it fast and then try the design on Ettin.

The speed half turned out to be done already: without ages written into each candidate's text, candidates go through the budget-wide reader at document-reader speed. What remained was mdo's design on row 222's ekmv start (arm emdo: 50 filing candidates per query), one seed on an L40S.
