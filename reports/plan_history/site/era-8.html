Row 91 · 2026-09-27 · EVAL-9 · §91 Trained behaviours hold across usersdone

Do the final recipe's trained behaviours hold for every user, not just one fold?

Why we ran it

Row 90 trained "the final recipe" (the training mix settled in row 89) four times, each time holding out a different quarter ("fold") of the 20 synthetic REAL-6 users, and showed headline accuracy held on all of them. But the special behaviours the recipe was built to teach had only been measured on fold 0. This row checks them on the other three folds.

What we tried

Scoring only, no new training. The behaviour test sets were rebuilt for all 20 users: misleading merchant names (the name suggests one category, the stored fact says another), the user's own override of a merchant, questions with no evidence at all (where the model should not be confident), and "alternation" rules (a user files one merchant two ways, by amount at stores or by weekday at restaurants). Each fold's model was read only on its own held-out users. The model is decider-4B, a 4-billion-parameter open model pre-trained to answer multiple-choice questions through a single answer slot.

Category: Gadgets & Food shopping

An alternation item: this user files Target two ways depending on the purchase; coined names like "Quorra" stop the model leaning on what a category is called. The options with their labels follow in the model's own layout.

What we learned

  • Every behaviour measured on fold 0 holds on the other folds within a few points.
  • The weak spot is the weekday rule at restaurants (64.6 to 68.8 per fold, against 86 to 96 for the amount rule). A set of days is harder to read off six rows than an amount threshold, and training drew day rules from only five fixed day sets.
  • One seed per fold, so small fold differences are within noise.

Questions it raised

  • Would more varied day sets in training teach the weekday rule in general? Tested in row 92.

Row 92 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-18 · §92 Random day sets vs weekend ruledone

Random weekday rules in training: does variety teach the day-of-week habit?

Why we ran it

Row 91 found the weekday rule at restaurants was the recipe's weakest behaviour (about 66% right). The guess was that training only ever used five fixed sets of days, too few for the model to learn "read whichever days this user singles out".

What we tried

The final recipe retrained with each weekday rule drawing its days as a random set of one to three weekdays (ALT_DAYS=rand) instead of the five fixed sets (three of which contain Friday or Saturday). Two seeds (independent training runs), fold 0. Read on the alternation set, REAL-6 and novel category names.

How it did

Reader (two seeds)Stores, observable rule (% right)Restaurants, observable rule (% right)Restaurants: probability on the right answer
Final recipe (fixed day sets)88.374.20.66
+ random day sets86.760.90.55

What we learned

  • The hypothesis was rejected: random day sets made the Friday/Saturday rule worse by 13 points.
  • Part of the fixed-set model's 74 was a prior ("weekend visits are the special ones"), which matches the test and plausibly real users. The general skill of reading arbitrary day rules from six rows is not learned at 800 steps with a 10% share of such episodes.
  • REAL-6 and novel names stayed within the seed spread. The recipe keeps the fixed day sets.

Questions it raised

  • Would more rows of the merchant in the prompt, or a larger share of day-rule episodes, teach the general rule? Not pursued directly; the history slices of row 98 later put more of the payee's own rows in the prompt.

Row 93 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-11 · §93 A more realistic synthetic populationdone

REAL-7: a messier, more realistic synthetic population

Why we ran it

Row 43 had asked for a successor to REAL-6 shaped like the real population, and on 2026-09-27 the owner asked for it. REAL-6 is tidy: custom category schemes, no mistakes, no changes. The final recipe of row 89 needed a harder, more lifelike check.

What we tried

build_real7.py wrote REAL-7 v1: 200 users no model had seen. About 55% use the default scheme ("Dining Out", "Transportation"), 30% lightly customise it (personal categories like "Date night"), 15% use fully custom schemes. A year of spending per user with the 24 most recent rows shown; merchants moved to a new category mid-year; categories created mid-year; 3% of history rows misfiled; idiosyncratic filings; short histories (0, 5 or 25 rows); a held-out family of bank statement styles. Each item records the best answer a reader could give from what the prompt shows, so the ceiling is 84.8%, not 100. Every REAL-6-trained decider-4B reader was scored zero-shot (no training on REAL-7).

Row 94 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-11 · §94 Misfiled rows fix training gapsdone

Training with misfiled rows: robust to slips, blind to real changes

Why we ran it

Row 93 suggested the final recipe was being pulled off course by a single misfiled history row, something its training data never contained.

What we tried

The final recipe with SHOT_NOISE=0.04: each example row ("shot") in a training prompt was refiled under another of the user's categories with probability 4%, and those wrong labels were excluded from the training loss so the model is not taught to predict them. Two seeds; read zero-shot on REAL-7 and on the REAL-6 sets.

How it did

Final recipe (row 89)82.095.385.957.545.9
+ shot noise 0.0482.297.594.835.838.8

What we learned

  • The hypothesis held: misfiled rows in training fixed ordinary items (95.3 to 97.5) and 25-row histories (85.9 to 94.8).
  • But the moved-merchant gain vanished (57.5 to 35.8). A genuine move looks like one or two recent rows under a new category, exactly what a misfile looks like, and the noise-trained model now discounts them.
  • Nothing elsewhere broke: REAL-6 88.3 / 86.6, override 98.1 / 99.9 across the two seeds.

Questions it raised

  • Can training show the difference between a move (the latest rows switch together) and a slip (one isolated row)? Tried in row 95.

Teaching "mistake or change?" with moves laid out in time

Why we ran it

Row 94's misfile training lost the merchants a user had genuinely moved. The idea: show training examples where a move is visible in time order, two early rows under the old category and the two latest under the new one.

What we tried

Row 94's recipe (misfiled shots at 4%) plus MOVE=0.1: 10% of training episodes laid out as a move, with the target following the new category. Two seeds; REAL-7, REAL-6, novel names, override, alternation.

Row 96 · 2026-09-27 · REAL-11 · §96 Misfiles among multiple rowsdone

Misfiles only where the majority is readable: the trade-off stays

Why we ran it

Row 95 concluded that random misfiles teach the model to distrust a lone unexpected row. The fix to try: misfile a row only when that merchant has three or more rows, so the majority still wins but a lone row is never a slip in training.

What we tried

The final recipe with SHOT_NOISE=0.15 SHOT_NOISE_MAJ=1: a shot is misfiled only if its merchant has three or more shots, at most one per merchant (about 4% of labels overall). Two seeds.

How it did

Reader (REAL-7, two seeds)All (% right)Ordinary items25-row historiesMerchant moved
Final recipe (row 89)82.095.385.957.5
+ shot noise 0.04 (row 94)82.297.594.835.8
+ majority-only misfiles82.197.392.739.2

What we learned

  • The ordinary-item fix stayed (97.3) and nothing else was lost (REAL-6 89.6 / 88.3, override 99.7 / 99.4), but moved merchants stayed lost (39.2).
  • Any noise training teaches "follow the majority of a merchant's filings"; a genuine move is the latest filings against an older majority. Without dates, the prompt cannot tell them apart.
  • The line was paused and the recipe stayed as row 89 (no noise), with the trade-off stated. Fixes would sit outside the prompt as built: dates on rows, the product recording a category change, or a recency rule in front of the model.

Questions it raised

  • Is the model's confidence at least shaken when filings conflict? Measured in row 97's review (§97 Confidence when filings change).
  • Would dated rows let the model see a change? Tested in row 98.

Row 97 · 2026-09-28 · EVAL-10 · §98 User effort and auto-filingdone

User effort: how much work does each auto-file policy save?

Why we ran it

A review on 2026-09-28 noted that on REAL-7 (row 93) the model's confident errors are items the prompt cannot answer. Accuracy alone does not say what a user would experience. Row 96 had also closed the misfile line, so the question became: given these models, what policy should decide which transactions to file automatically? (A companion analysis, §97 Confidence when filings change, found the final recipe's confidence drops usefully when a merchant's filings are mixed: 38% of such answers confident against 99% on consistent ones; it also corrected rows 95 and 96, whose merchant matching undercounted rows.)

What we tried

CPU only, over saved scores. A new "effort" metric per transaction: auto-filed and right costs 0; auto-filed and wrong costs 5 (notice, undo, refile; 3 and 10 also tried); otherwise the top three suggestions are shown, costing 1 click if the answer is among them and 3 (a search) if not. Policies: the model alone above a calibrated 98% confidence threshold (fitted on other users); the owner's history rule (auto-file when the user's last two filings of the merchant agree); history with the model agreeing; history or model.

How it did

Policy (REAL-7, final recipe)Auto-filed %Precision %Effort per transaction (wrong = 5)
Suggestions only0.0–1.22
Model at 98%2.795.41.20
History, last 2 agree, whole history60.194.50.67
History + model agrees53.994.40.79
History, or model at 98%60.994.50.67
REAL-6: history or model at 98%81.697.10.32 (from 1.02)

What we learned

  • REAL-6 overstated what model confidence can auto-file: 77% there, only 2.7% on REAL-7, because REAL-7's confident errors are unanswerable from the prompt.
  • The owner's last-two-filings rule carries auto-filing (60% at 94.5%); every error is a merchant whose filing changed or depends on the transaction.
  • Asking the model to agree with the history adds no safety: it agrees exactly where the history is stale.
  • History-or-model cuts effort by 45% on REAL-7 and 69% on REAL-6. The model's other value is the suggestion list (top three hold the answer 89% of the time on REAL-7).

Questions it raised

  • The rule sees the whole history; the model sees only 24 recent rows. Would giving the model the merchant's own dated rows make its confidence mean as much? Taken up in row 98.

Row 98 · 2026-09-29 · REAL-22, REAL-11, REAL-9 · §99 The history slice, zero-shot, §101done

Which slice of history to put in the prompt, and training on it

Why we ran it

Row 97 showed the model only sees the 24 latest rows, while the history rule uses everything. The owner (2026-09-28) asked for "the ultimate slice of history": this payee's rows, similar payees, recent rows, one row per category, dated and in date order, never a row from the query's future.

What we tried

Stage 1: build_real7_slices.py rebuilt REAL-7's 1,200 queries under ten slice policies (payee's own rows "self", similar payees "sim" by three similarity measures, one row per category "cover", recent rows, 24- or 48-row budgets, dated or not). Building it caught a leak (5 rows dated after their query, 23 rows under a not-yet-created category), now removed. The final recipe was read zero-shot. Stage 2: the recipe retrained with 2,400 extra episodes from 300 new REAL-7-style users in the sliced, dated layout.

Why we ran it

Users with more than 26 categories: training on all 255 labels

Why we ran it

Row 100 found 27% of blind_v1's items belong to users with 27 to 45 categories, beyond the A to Z labels the one-slot readers were trained with. Granite read those at 17.5; decider coped only because its own pre-training used 255 labels.

What we tried

The final recipe with LABELS=rand255 (each question draws its option labels at random from decider's 255 single-token labels, such as "AE" or "FX"), and a second arm adding EMPTY=20 (half the episodes get 1 to 20 extra made-up categories with no examples, so training questions also go past 26 options). decider-4B and Granite-4.0-micro, one seed each.

What we learned

  • A one-slot reader must be trained on as many labels as users have categories: Granite went from 17.5 to 83 on those items.
  • decider loses nothing with 255 labels and auto-files more of the blind set (51% to 57 to 58%).
  • Empty categories help novel names (90.9): they teach the reader not to rely on a category having examples.
  • The recipe took LABELS=rand255 EMPTY=20 (one seed). Granite still trails on stored facts and is not a replacement.

Dated-slice training that transfers, and the encoder-then-decoder split

Why we ran it

Row 100 showed that row 98's dated REAL-7 training helped changes of mind but wrecked auto-filing on the blind set. Row 102 made the encoder a full candidate, so a split (encoder for known payees, decoder for the rest) was worth measuring.

What we tried

The row text planned slices from two generators (REAL-7 and a second blind one). What ran instead (§106 A small dose of dated slices) was a test of dose and breadth: the recipe with 300, 600 or 1,200 dated slice episodes drawn from the 2,400-user REAL-7 pool, beside its 2,250 REAL-6 episodes, one seed each. Then the system (§107 The recipe settled by effort): Ettin-1B auto-files what clears its own 98% threshold and decider reads the rest, scored by user effort on blind_v1.

What we learned

  • The auto-file collapse came from the dose and narrowness (2,400 fixed episodes from 300 users), not from dates. 300 episodes from a wide pool keep auto-filing and lift changes of mind from 69 to 78.
  • More slices buy in-distribution REAL-7 accuracy and cost blind auto-filing.
  • The encoder filing first and decider reading the rest cut blind effort to 0.59 to 0.63 (decider alone about 0.69); decider reads 56% of transactions (detail in row 105).
