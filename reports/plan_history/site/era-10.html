Row 121 · 2026-09-29 · EVAL-13 · REPORT §116done

Does the 98% auto-file threshold hold for every kind of user?

Why we ran it

The scorecard from row 97 auto-files a transaction (files it without asking the user) only when the model's confidence passes a threshold chosen so that 98% of auto-filed items are right. Row 116 had just shown the models' confidence is a calibrated probability on average. A gaps review asked whether that one threshold still delivers 98% for sub-groups such as brand-new users, and whether it survives a move to a different population of users.

What we tried

CPU only, over saved scores on blind_v1 (the first test set written by an agent that never saw our training code). Two readers: decider-4B (a 4-billion-parameter language model fine-tuned with our recipe, two seeds) and Ettin-1B (a 1B encoder, three seeds). We measured realised precision by user group; fitted one threshold per group; tried "finite-sample" thresholds that come with a statistical guarantee (a bound on the error rate at 90% confidence); and carried thresholds fitted on other sets (REAL-6, novel names) over to blind_v1.

How it did

decider-4B, blind_v1auto-filed %right when auto-filed %
One threshold, all users63.397.9
One threshold, new users (0-10 rows)2192.7
Finite-sample guarantee (fixed sequence)32.798.8
Threshold carried over from REAL-640.798.8
Threshold carried over from novel_merchants_v10.8100.0

What we learned

  • The 98% holds on average but not for new users: 92.7% for decider and 89.3% for the encoder.
  • Per-group thresholds did not fix it here: the new-user group (168 items) is too small to place a threshold reliably.
  • A real guarantee costs coverage at about 1,100 calibration items: decider auto-files 33% instead of 63%.
  • Thresholds do not travel between populations; they moved coverage by 15 to 60 points. In production, fit them on the product's own recent confirmations.

Questions it raised

  • Is a fixed precision target the right way to decide at all? Row 126 replaced it with an expected-cost rule.
  • How should new users be treated: a stricter threshold, or none? Left for real data (row 144).

Row 122 · 2026-09-29 · TRAIN-13 · REPORT §124done

Can weight averaging make auto-filing stable across training seeds?

Why we ran it

Row 106 found that two training runs of the same recipe, differing only in the random seed, got the same accuracy but auto-filed very different shares (an 18-point spread). A product cannot ship a recipe whose behaviour depends on the seed.

What we tried

decider-4B with the labelled-rows recipe, seeds 0 to 2, trained plainly and with an exponential moving average (EMA, decay 0.99) of the LoRA adapter weights. EMA keeps a running average of the weights during training, which often smooths out seed noise. Each run was scored on blind_v1, REAL-6 and novel names, both at the fixed 98% cut and under the per-item rule of row 126.

How it did

Weightsblind top-1 spreadauto-filed spread, 98% cut (points)auto-filed spread, per-item rule W=10 (points)mean effort, per-item rule
Plain, 3 seeds0.74.81.70.659
EMA 0.99, 3 seeds0.66.31.90.682

What we learned

  • EMA does not help: accuracy is unchanged, auto-filing is lower and no steadier, and user effort is higher.
  • Most of the instability came from the fixed threshold, which sits on the steep part of each seed's confidence curve. Under the per-item rule the plain recipe's auto-filing varies only 1.7 points across seeds.
  • Decision: no EMA; the per-item rule is also the stability fix.

Questions it raised

  • None new; seed spread is reported under the per-item rule from here on.

Row 123 · 2026-09-30 · MODEL-19 · REPORT §136done

Can the large model teach the small one?

Why we ran it

Row 120 showed the untrained decider-35B-A3B (a 35-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 3B active per token) knows far more about new users and new businesses than the trained 4B. If so, its probability spread ("soft targets") might teach the 4B some of that knowledge during training, a technique called distillation.

What we tried

Per seed, the training episodes where the payee has never appeared in the user's history (about 1,100 of 2,250) were read by the untrained 35B. decider-4B was then trained with the recipe and the other-users line, with the target on those episodes set to half the true label and half the 35B's distribution. Two seeds.

How it did

decider-4Bblind top-1 %first-time payees right 1st %effort W=10 (lower is better)REAL-6 %
Other-users recipe (row 124)83.859.90.62287.8

What we learned

  • The teacher was right on only 38 to 39% of these training episodes: they use synthetic merchants and coined category names it cannot know, so half of each target pointed away from the label.
  • The student ended where it started. Distillation is dropped for synthetic training.
  • Where the 35B's knowledge is valid (real businesses), the recipe already has exact labels; a teacher would matter on real statement strings without labels.

Questions it raised

  • Are the 35B's readings of real payees useful soft labels? Listed for real data (row 144); a related idea, distilling the 35B's payee knowledge into a smaller model, became row 170.

Row 126 · 2026-09-29 · EVAL-14 · REPORT §118done

Scoring without an arbitrary threshold

Why we ran it

The owner: "The 98% threshold is arbitrary. Let's come up with a better mechanism for scoring and determining success." Row 121 had also shown the threshold is fragile. Calibrated probabilities from row 116 made a cost-based rule possible.

What we tried

Success is the user's expected effort per transaction: 0 for a right auto-file, 1 click if the answer is among three suggestions, 3 for a search, and W for a wrong auto-file (noticing and undoing it). W is a product judgement. For each transaction the rule auto-files when the expected cost of auto-filing, (1 − p) × W, is below the expected cost of suggesting. No threshold to tune. Compared with the fixed 98% cut and the best possible cut, for every reader and combination on blind_v1, W from 3 to 100. CPU only.

How it did

Effort per transaction (no model = 3.0)W = 3W = 10W = 50
decider-4B, fixed 98%0.5660.6621.208
decider-4B, per-item rule0.4810.6510.955
encoder × decider × 35B, per-item rule0.3970.5470.923

What we learned

  • The per-item rule is best or tied at every W for every reader, with nothing to tune.
  • The old 98% target corresponds to W of about 10: at W = 10 the rule auto-files 62.5% at 98.1% precision.
  • The ranking of readers and systems is the same at every W, so conclusions do not hinge on the exact W.
  • The rule is only as good as the calibration, which must be fitted on the product's own users.

Questions it raised

  • What is the product's W? The owner's call, later measurable from undo rates (row 144).
  • Does the score match how the owner thinks about suggestions? Row 132 built a rank-based score on the owner's scale.

Row 125's adapter targets (experts still frozen) with twice the learning rate (2e-4 × 800 steps) and with twice the steps (1e-4 × 1,600). Adding LoRA to the routed experts was ruled out: rank 64 over 256 experts per layer is billions of parameters. One H200 each.

What we tried

Row 125's trained 35B (adapter merged) in vLLM 0.30 on one H200, on the same 24 user syncs and token ids as row 127, with the prefix cache off and on. Training times and costs read from the job logs.

Row 132 · 2026-09-30 · EVAL-14 · REPORT §129done

A rank-based score on the owner's scale

Why we ran it

The owner wanted the score to reflect a suggestion list: "guessing correctly in 1st place is a 0. Being 1 away is a 1 ... up to 5. And not showing it is a 10", with irrelevant suggestions penalised and only confident ones shown. Row 126's effort score only counted "in the top three or not".

What we tried

CPU only, blind_v1. A new score: the right category at rank r costs r − 1, not shown costs 10, each wrong category shown costs a clutter penalty (LAM), at most five shown. Each rank is shown only if its expected gain beats its expected clutter, so list length follows from the calibrated probabilities. Auto-filing is chosen when cheaper than the list; a "confirm cost" for tapping a suggestion was added after a first run.

How it did

System (W = 20, LAM 1, confirm 0.5)score (no model = 10)auto-filed % / precision %suggestions shown
decider-4B + line1.76556.1 / 98.31.75
encoder × decider1.67061.8 / 98.11.88
encoder × decider × 35B1.49663.9 / 98.41.82

What we learned

  • The ranking is good: right first 84 to 86%, in the top five 95 to 96%.
  • On this scale a right first suggestion costs the same as a right auto-file, so the rule never auto-files unless confirming costs something. A confirm cost of 0.5 (a tap) restores the trade.
  • Lists are short when the model is sure (1.6 to 2.0 suggestions). Proposed defaults: miss 10, clutter 1, confirm 0.5, W 20.

Questions it raised

  • The clutter penalty treats every wrong suggestion alike; "plausible but wrong" should be free. Row 133.

Why we ran it

The same outside review found that options described by what they lead to matter a great deal. A personal category name like "Yum" says little; listing the payees filed under it might help. The baseline is row 124's recipe.

Each category in the labelled list followed by the payees the user filed under it (DESC=1). decider-4B trained that way (two seeds), and row 124's adapter reading the described list without training on it.

Row 139 · 2026-09-30 · EVAL-14 · REPORT §134done

How many suggestions to show

Why we ran it

Row 138 found that showing five suggestions for first-time payees would find the right category far more often than the short confident list.

What we tried

CPU only, with YNAB's suggestion first where it has one. Five list policies: current (plausible categories plus confident ones), adaptive (current for known payees, top five for first-time payees), a probability floor (p ≥ 0.05 or 0.1), and always five. On blind_v1 and the ordinary stream.

How it did

Policyblind_v1 first-time: in the list %stream first-time: in the list %stream: suggestions per transaction
Current80.961.21.57
Adaptive88.176.11.70
p ≥ 0.0586.369.41.56
Always five88.176.15.00

What we learned

  • Adaptive is the product choice: five for first-time payees, the short list otherwise. First-time payees are only 4% of an ordinary stream, so the average list barely grows.
  • A floor of 0.05 is a middle ground if five looks cluttered.
  • Always five adds 1 to 2 points on known payees at 3.5 more wrong suggestions each: not worth it.
