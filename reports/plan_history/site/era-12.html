Row 151 · 2026-10-01 · MODEL-22 · §146 strands-decider, an outside modeldone

Trying AWS's new open "decider" model, strands-decider, on our task

Why we ran it

The owner found a newly published open-source decision model from AWS (strands-decider, Apache-2.0) and asked how it handles our datasets compared with our best reader, decider-4B trained with "the recipe" (our standard set of training episodes). It has a different design: a Qwen3.5-2B-Base body with its word-predicting head thrown away, and a small (~1M-parameter) "pointer head" that scores each option by comparing the hidden state at the answer position with the hidden state at the end of each option's line.

What we tried

First we checked we were using it correctly: their engine on their README example, their own evaluation on their held-out rows, our reader against theirs on 61 questions (same answer on all 61), and an easy ladder of obvious transactions. Then we scored the published v19 checkpoint untrained, and trained it on the recipe's 12,800 episode draws three ways: continuing v19 in our loop, a fresh strands head on Qwen3.5-2B-Base in our loop, and strands' own trainer and config. decider-2B with the recipe was the same-size comparison. Each trained arm took about 22 minutes on one H100.

A synthetic blind_v2 user (trimmed) wrapped in strands' own prompt: our whole item is the "state", the category names are the options.

  • Used correctly, strands reproduces its published numbers, but untrained it barely reads our task (blind_v1 25.9% without the other-users line).

Row 152 · 2026-10-01 · MODEL-23 · §147 What decider borrows from strandsdone

What decider can borrow from strands: the training signal, not the head

Why we ran it

Row 151 left two explanations for strands' failure on invented category names: its pointer readout, or the fact that it lacked our shot-label loss. And at 2B strands was better than decider on first-time payees. The owner asked whether decider could learn anything from strands' architecture.

What we tried

Two experiments. (1) A strands-style pointer added on top of decider's label scores (decider-2B and -4B, two seeds), reading the options listed again after the transaction, plus a control with the relisted options but no pointer. (2) strands' architecture reading our labelled history layout (each category given a random code that appears in the list and in each history row), with and without the shot-label loss. All arms used the recipe's episodes, 800 steps of 16.

Transaction: 2025-11-12 | UBER *TRIP HELP.UBER.COM CA | $35.19 | Wed

Options:
- (xf) Groceries
- (Kq) Getting around
...

The labelled layout from a synthetic blind_v1 user, with the options relisted after the query so a pointer can read them (codes illustrative; they are random per episode).

How it did

What we learned

  • The shot-label loss is what teaches invented names, whatever the head: with it, strands jumps from 64.1 to 86.6 on REAL-6, matching decider-2B.
  • A pointer adds nothing to decider beyond seed noise at 2B or 4B. Not adopted.
  • strands' first-time-payee edge at 2B shrank once it had the shot-label loss (one seed: a hint of a trade-off).

Questions it raised

  • Would strands at 4B with the shot-label loss catch decider-4B, and do strands' other choices (LoRA targets, rank) help? Row 153.

strands at 4B, and strands' adapter settings on decider

Why we ran it

After row 152 showed the shot-label loss was the missing ingredient, the owner asked to "try using a 4B torso with an equivalently sized head or borrow anything else from strands".

What we tried

Three arms, two seeds each, all on the recipe's episodes: (A) strands' architecture on Qwen3.5-4B-Base with its pointer head widened in proportion (dim 320, ~1.6M parameters), our labelled layout and the shot-label loss; (B) decider-4B with strands' extra LoRA targets (two projections in the Gated DeltaNet layers); (C) decider-4B with strands' smaller LoRA rank, 16 instead of our 64. We also multiplied the probabilities of decider-4B and strands-4B to see whether they make different mistakes.

decider's training method on strands' own datasets

Why we ran it

After row 153, decider's readout looked strong and cheap to train. The owner asked for a test training on strands' datasets to compare our results with theirs, as a research comparison only.

What we tried

We rebuilt strands' v19 training corpus with their own scripts (every file matched their recorded checksums; 123,339 training rows of public classification, multi-step document questions and answer-adequacy tasks). From the same Qwen3.5-2B-Base, we trained decider's "one-slot" readout (options labelled, the answer read as a label token from the language-model head; LoRA rank 64), keeping everything about strands' data recipe. Two seeds, one 85-minute run each on one H100. We scored with strands' own evaluation and re-scored v19 to confirm the harness.

Row 155 · 2026-10-02 · MODEL-24 · §150 Combining both readout methodsdone

A hybrid head for strands' own tasks

Why we ran it

Row 154 showed each readout winning on some tasks (decider's on multi-hop and ordered scores, strands' on emotion). The owner asked whether any of our improvements could help strands, and agreed to test first whether the two complement each other, then a hybrid head.

What we tried

(1) Multiply v19's and decider's calibrated per-question probabilities. (2) A hybrid head: decider's label scores plus a strands-style pointer over the option lines, trained together on strands' corpus exactly as in row 154, two seeds.

How it did

Set (% right)v19decider's methodv19 × deciderhybrid head
held-out short tasks (6,000)64.065.266.265.9
emotion56.454.356.454.7
multi-step (4,084)82.783.984.084.6

What we learned

  • The two readouts complement each other modestly: each gets 20 to 45% of the other's misses right.
  • One hybrid model captures most of the gain (65.9 held-out, both seeds above decider's) and the best multi-step score, but not v19's lead on emotion.
  • On our categoriser the same hybrid added nothing (row 152); under a point is not worth an extra head that cannot be merged into the weights. Not adopted; MODEL-24 answered.

Questions it raised

  • None carried forward on strands; attention moved to decider's hidden states as embeddings (row 157).

Row 156 · 2026-10-02 · TRAIN-14 · §151 Adapter capacity isn't the limitdone

Does the model need more trainable capacity? Bigger adapters and full fine-tunes

Why we ran it

Row 153 found that shrinking the LoRA adapter to rank 16 cost up to 6 points where the history decides. The owner asked whether we should be trying full fine-tunes. If rank 16 was short, maybe our rank 64 was too.

What we tried

The decider-4B recipe with LoRA rank 128 and 256 (H100), and every weight trained (full fine-tune) at learning rates 1e-5 and 3e-5 with full-precision master weights (H200, 80.5 GiB). One seed per new arm, against the recipe's three seeds. Judged on the blind sets, first-time payees (the check for forgetting general knowledge), the behaviour sets, and REAL-7 as a diagnostic only.

How it did

Readerblind_v1 + lineblind_v2 + linefirst-time payees (blind_v1)new users (blind_v1)REAL-6

What we learned

  • Rank 128 equals rank 64: capacity is not binding.
  • More capacity forgets what the base model knew about merchants and people: first-time payees and new users drop sharply, while payees seen before hold.
  • REAL-7, built with our own generators, hides the loss (full 1e-5 scores 81.9 against 82.2), which is why it is only a diagnostic. Rank 64 stays.

Questions it raised

  • With real data (a billion filings) the balance may change; repeat this probe then, judged on held-out real users and first-time payees.

Row 157 · 2026-10-02 · MODEL-25 · §152 decider's hidden states as embeddingsdone

Using decider's hidden states as embeddings of payees and categories

Why we ran it

The owner asked whether we could extract embeddings (numeric vectors that put similar things close together) from decider and cluster with them. Row 150 had grouped payees and categories with crude text features (TF-IDF); a better embedding could replace them.

What we tried

We embedded about 420 payees (kindpay_v2, three renderings each) and 2,867 user categories (kindcat_v2: the name plus up to 8 payees filed under it). Readers: decider-4B untrained and with the recipe, its base Qwen3.5-4B-Base, the purpose-built Qwen3-Embedding-4B, and a no-model TF-IDF baseline. For the decoders we read the hidden state at the last token after a short cue, or averaged over tokens, at half, three-quarter and full depth. Judged against the generators' true kinds: nearest-neighbour accuracy (for categories, only neighbours with a different name count) and k-means cluster agreement (NMI).

Example prompt

Budget category "All the food". Payees filed under it: Etsy, PayPal, Whataburger, Publix, McDonald's, ...
In one word, the kind of spending:

A synthetic kindcat_v2 category with the cue; the embedding is the hidden state at the cue's last token.

Personal embeddings: letting a user's history shift the vectors

Why we ran it

After row 157, the owner said: "I definitely want a user's history to perturb the embeddings! Of both their categories and the payees!"

What we tried

Same read-out as row 157, but with the user's history slice placed in front of each text ("personal") or not ("global"), for each of the user's categories and for the transaction to file. An embedding-only categoriser files the transaction under the nearest category, in every pairing of personal and global. Compared with decider-4B's own answer and YNAB's rule on blind_v1 and blind_v2, split by first-time payees, known payees, person-to-person payments and new users.

Row 159 · 2026-10-02 · MODEL-25 · §154 Mapping categories across usersdone

Mapping categories with embeddings: between users, and onto a standard list

Why we ran it

The owner asked whether embeddings could map canonical categories onto a person's own set, or map two users' categories together. Row 158 supplied personal embeddings, and the multiple-choice answers from row 152 and row 153 gave a comparison.

What we tried

On kindcat_v2 (blind_v2's users, 2,867 categories): (1) each user category to the nearest of 43 canonical options, against decider asked the multiple-choice question; (2) 600 user pairs, each of A's single-kind categories to B's nearest. Embeddings of the name, the name with filed payees, and personal ones, from decider-4B (untrained and recipe), Qwen3-Embedding-4B and TF-IDF.

How it did

TaskMethod% right first
A's category → B's, different namesno model (TF-IDF)23.5
A's category → B's, different namesQwen3-Embedding-4B69.9
A's category → B's, different namesdecider-4B recipe, personal82.3
category → canonical kinddecider-4B recipe, nearest (personal)49.1
category → canonical kinddecider-4B untrained, asked57.8

What we learned

  • Across users, embeddings work: 85.4% overall, 82.3% when the names differ.
  • Onto canonical kinds, asking beats embedding. Embeddings fail on "several kinds" and "person, trip or purpose" categories (0.6 to 17.5%), which lie near no single kind label.
  • Use embeddings to align users' categories; use the question to label a category with a kind.

Questions it raised

  • Embedding work then turned to real data: similar-payee rows on the owner's budget (row 166) and payee matching (row 171).

Row 160 · 2026-10-02 · DATA-8 · proposedtodo

Payee resolution on synthetic strings: which corrupted names are the same payee?

Why we ran it

The owner pointed out that real payee strings are obscured in many ways (truncation, transaction ids, locations), and that YNAB's real data does not carry enough aggregator merchant fields to skip cleanup. Row 150's payee keys grouped strings with high purity but missed about a sixth of them.

What we tried

Proposed, awaiting the owner. Gold would come from pop_v2's generator payee ids (2,250 users, 2.6M rows) plus a corrupter at controlled strengths: truncation, transaction ids, locations, processor prefixes, typos. Methods, cheapest first: extended key rules; blocking by first token with prefix-aware and character n-gram matching; embeddings cued for identity ("The business name in this bank line is:"); decider as a yes/no judge on borderline pairs; co-filing behaviour (same users, same categories, similar amounts). Measures: pairwise precision, recall and F1, and B-cubed, by corruption type and strength.

Questions it raised

  • The question was taken up on real strings labelled by YNAB instead: row 171, then as a trained task in row 176.
