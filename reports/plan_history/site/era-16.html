Row 201 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §180 Where decider and encoders disagreedone

Do decider and the small encoders make the same mistakes?

Why we ran it

Row 199 found that, with fair settings, adding the history encoders to decider gained only +0.3. Whether any combination can ever pay depends on how different the two readers' errors are. Pinterest's Multi-Embedding Retrieval paper got a big gain from combining two retrievers because they shared only 3.2% of their candidates. After reading the Pinterest papers, the owner asked us to "apply all of their learnings to our approach".

What we tried

For every transaction we recorded who got it right: decider only, the encoder only (kNN or MaxSim from row 195), both, or neither. We split the counts by how often the payee had been filed before and by the size of the right category. We also tested rules a real system could use to pick between them: "the more confident reader wins", and a gate whose thresholds were chosen on synthetic households. Reads ran on 100 held-out synthetic households and the owner's budget (aggregates only), in 3 minutes on the new GPU readers from row 208.

Example prompt

Transaction: SQ *LANTERN BOOKS | $18.40 | Tue
decider top choice:  Gifts        (p 0.48)   wrong
MaxSim top choice:   Books & Media (p 0.61)  right
-> counted as "only the encoder right"

Illustrative, invented: one transaction's tally. The question is how often this case happens, and whether any signal can spot it.

What we learned

  • The errors overlap heavily: where decider is wrong, an encoder is right only about one time in five (4.6 of 27.5 points).
  • A perfect chooser would gain +4.3 to +4.6, but no confidence signal finds those cases: the best rule stays at decider's 72.5.
  • decider's lead is largest where history is thin (small categories, first-time payees); the encoders nearly match it where history is rich. For now the encoders' role is cost, not accuracy.

Questions it raised

  • Can the encoders get better on their own? (row 202, row 204, row 205)
  • Can decider's own history and speed improve? (row 206)
  • Does a model where history and options interact close the gap? (row 211)

Row 202 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §182 Encoder training fixes from the papersdone

Training the history encoder with Pinterest's tricks

Why we ran it

Pinterest's papers report big gains from changes to how a retrieval encoder is trained: correcting for popular items, adding many random wrong answers, never treating a near-duplicate as a wrong answer, and scoring against the user's own set of choices. The owner asked to "apply all of their learnings". Row 194's encoder was the starting point.

What we tried

We rebuilt row 194's training in a plain loop and checked it reproduced the old encoder (b0). Then one seed each of: b1 dedup (no wrong answer from the same household and category, at most 400 examples per payee); b2 b1 plus logQ, a discount for popular items; b3 b1 plus 1,024 random wrong answers per step drawn from all households; b3b the same with same-payee ones removed; b4 the household softmax, where the loss is the MaxSim reader itself over the household's categories; b5 the old loss plus dedup plus the household softmax. Each was read on 50 held-out households and on decider's own items.

Example prompt

ANCHOR:     Fernway Hardware | $22.18 | Sat
POSITIVE:   Pine Lumber Co   | $64.00  (filed: Home Repair)
NEGATIVES:  Fernway Hardware | $9.40   (filed: Garden)      hard negative
            [masked] Fernway Hardware | $31.10 (filed: Home Repair)  dedup: same category, not a negative

Illustrative, invented: a triplet with b1's dedup rule. A row filed in the anchor's own category is no longer counted as a wrong answer.

How it did

50 held-out households, % right first (kNN | MaxSim)All (359,050)First-time payee (53,479)
Old encoder (row 194)74.5 | 73.460.0 | 59.2
b1 dedup, payee cap74.5 | 73.460.0 | 59.2
b2 + logQ73.8 | 72.955.6 | 56.0
b3 + random-negative pool61.1 | 57.838.8 | 34.4
b4 household softmax alone74.4 | 73.159.9 | 58.8

On the owner's budget, over decider's options: b5 64.6 | 64.7 against the old encoder's 63.6 | 64.8; decider 72.5. Aggregates only.

What we learned

  • None of the fixes moves the encoder: everything sits within 0.3 of the old one. They solve web-scale problems that a household's 30 to 60 categories do not have.
  • logQ hurts first-time payees by about 4 points: it discounts popular payees, which are exactly the neighbours a new payee should be compared with.
  • The random-negative pool collapsed to chance during training. That is a bug to find, not a verdict on the idea.
  • The gap to decider is not in the training objective; it is in what the model sees and what it knows.

Questions it raised

  • Does a scorer that sees the history as a sequence do better? (row 203)
  • Does the crowd's naming help the encoder? (row 205)
  • Does a decision-model design change the picture? (row 210)

Row 203 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

A small sequence scorer built the way Pinterest's TransAct really works

Why we ran it

Pinterest's TransAct ranker got most of its gain not from its transformer but from details around it: marking what kind of action each history row was, pasting the candidate onto every history row, and a simple read-out. Row 195's TransAct-style query had read 65.0, below MaxSim's 69.6. The owner's "apply all of their learnings" asked for a faithful version.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. For each transaction and each candidate category, build a short sequence: the household's nearest earlier filings, the most recent ones, and the candidate category's own nearest filings. Each token holds the filing's vector, its category's vector, the candidate's vector, their similarity, its age and amount features. A tiny transformer (2 layers, 1 head, width 64) scores it, with a softmax over the household's categories and random time-window masking in training. Compared against row 195's query (65.0) and MaxSim (69.6).

Example prompt

Candidate: Dining Out
  [Harbor Noodle   | Dining Out | cand=Dining Out | cos 0.82 | 3 d  | $24]
  [Corner Deli     | Lunch      | cand=Dining Out | cos 0.61 | 1 d  | $11]
  [Rosie's Diner   | Dining Out | cand=Dining Out | cos 0.58 | 40 d | $42]
-> score(Dining Out)

Illustrative, invented: each bracket stands for one token built from vectors and numbers, not text the model reads.

Questions it raised

  • Does pasting the candidate onto each history row beat MaxSim, as it beat simpler readers at Pinterest?
  • Could a pretrained sequence model start this scorer? (row 207)

Row 204 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §183 Several prototypes per categorydone

Summing up each category with a few representative purchases

Why we ran it

Row 194 found that one average vector per category is weak, and row 195 that comparing with every filing (MaxSim) is strong. Pinterest's PinnerSage sits between: it clusters a user's history into a few interests and keeps one representative for each. That might keep the gain of full history at lower cost, and handle categories that hold several kinds of thing.

What we tried

No training: we reused row 194's encoder vectors. For each category we clustered its earlier filings (Ward clustering, up to 20 clusters) and represented each cluster by its medoid (the most central real filing) or its centroid (the average). One arm weighted each cluster by its size, optionally decayed by age. Another used farthest-point prototypes (3 or 5 spread-out filings), from the Multi-Embedding Retrieval paper. A category scored as its best prototype's similarity. Read on 50 held-out households.

Example prompt

Category "Kids": 140 filings -> 4 clusters
  medoid 1: Little Steps Daycare | $310   (52 filings)
  medoid 2: Splash Swim School   | $45    (30 filings)
  medoid 3: Toy Barn             | $27    (41 filings)
  medoid 4: Pediatric Partners   | $35    (17 filings)
Transaction: Splash Swim School | $45 -> best match: medoid 2

Illustrative, invented: a category reduced to four representatives, weighted by cluster size.

How it did

Reader (50 held-out households, % top-1 / top-3)All (359,050)First-time payee (53,479)
MaxSim over every earlier filing73.4 / 90.259.2 / 79.8
Ward medoids + cluster size68.8 / 86.653.5 / 74.5
Ward medoids + size, decayed by age (lambda 0.05)63.9 / 81.550.8 / 70.8
Ward centroids64.2 / 88.253.4 / 78.3
Ward medoids52.9 / 82.244.0 / 72.1
Farthest-point, 3 prototypes42.6 / 72.941.0 / 67.8

What we learned

  • Compressing a category loses what makes it work: the best prototype reader trails MaxSim by 4.6, farthest-point by 30.
  • PinnerSage compresses thousands of pins to save serving cost; a household category has tens to hundreds of filings, which MaxSim reads in full in seconds, so there is nothing to save.
  • Cluster size matters more than recency: weighting by size recovers most of the loss, decaying by age only hurts.

Questions it raised

  • Prototypes stay out of the late-interaction model, which also does best seeing the full history (row 210).
  • The clustering was CPU work billed on an H100 for two hours; CPU jobs now take --gpu cpu.

Row 205 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

Giving the encoder the crowd's names for a payee

Why we ran it

In Pinterest's OmniSearchSage, the board titles other users gave an item (their own names for its collections) were worth +17% beside the item's own text. Our equivalent is the crowd: the category names other households use for the same bank string, which already helps decider as the crowd line. The owner asked to "apply all of their learnings", so this row puts the crowd inside the encoder's text.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. Append the top crowd category names (deduplicated, ranked by frequency then specificity) to a bank string's text when training and reading the history encoders. A second, cheap arm: a hashed n-gram tower (words, word pairs and three-letter runs) as a payee encoder. Synthetic households first, since the owner's budget has no crowd data.

Example prompt

SQ *BLUE KETTLE CAFE | $6.85 | Tue | crowd: Coffee, Eating out, Treats

Illustrative, invented: the transaction text the encoder would read, with the crowd's three most common names appended.

Questions it raised

  • Does the crowd help an encoder as much as it helps decider (+7 on first-time payees)?
  • The crowd line was later carried into the late-interaction model's training (§198 decider remaining training lessons in the late-interaction model: +6 on first-time payees where a crowd exists).

Row 206 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27 · proposedtodo

Choosing decider's history rows more cleverly, and scoring every category at once

Why we ran it

decider shows the 4B model about 24 recent household rows. Pinterest's TransAct V2 and PinnerSage choose history from the whole record instead: rows nearest to the current item, the most recent, and one per interest cluster. UniPinRec found a longer history beat a deeper model per unit of compute. Row 201 showed decider's lead is largest where history is thin, so the history it sees matters.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. (a) History rows chosen from the whole history (nearest plus most recent plus one representative per cluster) against today's 24. (b) A longer history. (c) One-pass scoring: cache the household's history once and score every category from it in one pass, as Pinterest's PinFM does, measuring tokens per second and cost per transaction.

Example prompt

Payee: Fernway Hardware | $22.18 | Sat
Nearest earlier rows:  Fernway Hardware | $9.40 | Garden ...
Recent rows:           Pine Lumber Co | $64.00 | Home Repair ...
One per interest:      Splash Swim School | $45 | Kids ...
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: arm (a)'s three kinds of history row in one prompt.

Questions it raised

  • Does a smarter history slice move the owner's budget, where prompt changes had stopped moving it?
  • How cheap can one-pass scoring make decider per transaction?

Row 207 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposedtodo

A small model pretrained on whole filing histories

Why we ran it

Pinterest's PinFM pretrains a sequence model on users' action histories and then fine-tunes it for ranking. A household's filings are a similar sequence: each purchase and where it went. If a model learns the shape of filing histories first, it may need less data to learn a household's habits. PinFM's ID tables broke on new items, so ours would read content, not IDs.

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. A 4 to 6 layer causal transformer over synthetic households' filing sequences. Each token is a payee text vector, amount and date features, and the filed category. It is pretrained to predict the next, several next, and future categories over the household's categories plus the 64 shared purpose clusters, then fine-tuned with the new transaction appended. It is compared with the same model trained from scratch. Sweeps on Modal.

Example prompt

[Maple Grocers $57 Fri -> Groceries]
[Fern St Pharmacy $14 Sat -> Health]
[Harbor Noodle $24 Sat -> Dining Out]
[Maple Grocers $61 Tue -> ?]

Illustrative, invented: a filing sequence; pretraining asks the model to fill in the next category.

Questions it raised

  • Does pretraining on synthetic histories help, or does it learn the generator's quirks (as row 216 later warned)?

Row 208 · 2026-10-05 · INFRA · §181 Faster runs: training and readsdone

Making every experiment faster and cheaper

Why we ran it

Each train-and-read job on Modal took about 103 minutes, and reading the history encoders took two minutes per household. The owner said "start on all of these efficiency changes before just about anything else. We've gotta make our loop tighter/faster!" and "Let's not duplicate work here!". A workflow review listed what to change first.

What we tried

Each change was checked against the path it replaced before use: the encoder readers moved to the GPU with a score cache; decider reads moved to vLLM, a fast serving engine, with one engine per job; training batches were grouped by length so less padding is computed; a generator now writes each row's job list with a cost estimate; and a watcher pulls results back and files them automatically. We also checked whether fewer training steps would do.

Example prompt

make_jobs.py specs/r2xx.yaml
  job r2xx-a1  train 800 steps + read 3 sets   est. 58 min  ~$4.0
  job r2xx-a2  train 800 steps + read 3 sets   est. 58 min  ~$4.0

Illustrative, invented: the kind of summary the job-list generator prints before anything launches.

How it did

ChangeCheck against the old pathSpeed
Encoder readers on the GPUsame top-1 on 11,373 / 11,373124 s to 2.1 s per household
vLLM reads, one engine per job99.68% same first choice (all differences near-ties)56 to 24.7 ms per item
Length-sorted training groups72.62 vs 72.37 (+0.25 ± 0.26, same model)5.2 to 3.8 s per step (-28%)
Fewer steps: 600 / 400 instead of 800-0.42 / -1.47 on the synthetic test set39 / 27 min instead of 50

A typical train-and-read job fell from about 103 to about 58 minutes.

What we learned

  • Roughly half the H100 cost per arm, before the one-seed-to-screen rule halves the number of arms.
  • 800 training steps stay the recipe: screening at fewer steps would mislead.
  • vLLM's speedup only pays when the engine's 2.3-minute start is shared across reads.
  • On the way we found and fixed a tracker bug that had dropped run records.

Questions it raised

  • Which Modal GPU is cheapest for the small encoder models? (row 219)
