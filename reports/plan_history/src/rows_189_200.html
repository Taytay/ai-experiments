Row 189 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §173 Two-hop similar-payee selection

Finding look-alike payees by what other people do with them

Why we ran it

When a payee is new to a household, the model is shown a few of the household's own earlier payees that look similar, so it can see where "things like this" go. Until now "similar" meant similar spelling. The owner proposed a second route through other people's behaviour: "payee X is often categorized as category clusters x, y, and z, and clusters x, y, and z are the way payees a, b, and c are often categorized." Row 187's two-tower encoder (one model that turns payees and categories into vectors, so matching ones sit close together) had been too weak to file transactions itself, but its category side could group categories from different households.

What we tried

We embedded every category of 800 synthetic households (its name plus the three payees last filed in it) and grouped them into 64 purpose clusters, so "Dining out", "Eating out" and "Takeout & restaurants" land together. Each bank string then got a profile: how many other households filed it in each cluster. The household's own earlier payees with the most similar profile became the similar rows, blended with the spelling ranking. This is two hops: payee to purposes, purposes back to payees. Nothing was retrained and the prompt format did not change. Two decider models read the sets, two seeds each.

Example prompt

Payee: SQ *HARBOR NOODLE 4471 | $23.40 | Sat
Similar earlier payees:
  Pho Lantern        | $31.10 | Dining Out
  Golden Wok Takeout | $18.75 | Dining Out
  Rosie's Diner      | $42.00 | Date Night
Which category?

Illustrative, invented: the three rows were chosen because other households file all four strings into the same purpose clusters, not because they are spelled alike.

Row 194 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §177 History-aware encoders

Small encoders that compare a purchase with the household's own past purchases

Why we ran it

Row 187's two-tower encoder matched a transaction against a description of each category and got only 47.2% right on the owner's budget. The owner suggested to "embed the historical similar transactions", and to "try all 3 approaches ... on its own, or to enhance an LLM prediction".

What we tried

We fine-tuned a small open text encoder (bge-small) so that a transaction sits close to earlier transactions filed in the same category. Training used InfoNCE: the model is shown a transaction, one right partner and many wrong ones, and is rewarded for picking the right one. The right partner was an earlier transaction in the same category, half the time from another payee. The hard wrong one was the most similar-looking earlier transaction filed somewhere else. We read the encoder three ways. (1) A kNN vote: the 20 nearest earlier transactions vote for their categories. (2) Prototypes: each category becomes the average of what was filed in it. (3) The query also carries its 5 nearest earlier transactions and where they went. Each was used alone and fused with decider: decider's score plus a weight W times the encoder's.

Example prompt

ANCHOR:   Maple Grocers | $57.09 | Fri
POSITIVE: Brookshire's | $83.40 | Sat   (filed: Groceries)
NEGATIVE: Maple Grocers | $141.02 | Sun (filed: Party Supplies)

Illustrative, invented: one training triplet. The negative has the same payee but went elsewhere, so the encoder must learn more than spelling.

Row 195 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §177 History-aware encoders

Pinterest-style history and late interaction for the encoders

Why we ran it

After row 194 the owner asked: "Is it possible to also try the late interaction models? ... Especially the Pinterest stuff." Pinterest's TransAct V2 got its biggest gains by changing which past actions the ranker sees: the most recent few plus the nearest few, chosen per candidate.

What we tried

We built three more readers. (4) TransAct: the query carries the 5 most recent and the 5 nearest earlier filings, with where each went and how long ago (elapsed time comes from Zepto's system). This is late interaction: compare many small pieces, keep the best matches. (6) ColBERT: late interaction one level down, at the word-piece level, used to re-rank encoder 1's 50 nearest. Each was read alone and fused with decider.

Row 196 · 2026-10-04 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · proposed

A running summary of each household that shifts where payees go

Why we ran it

The history encoders in row 194 and row 195 see past filings one at a time. The owner, thinking like a recommender system, wanted "incorporating recent knowledge ... to perturb the mappings". On a trip, the same coffee shop may belong under the trip's category. After the owner reorganises categories, old filings point the wrong way. Pinterest recomputes a user's embedding on every request for the same reason: people "change their status instantly".

What we tried

Proposed, not yet run. A small user tower (a network that turns the household's last N filings into one state vector) reads each filing's encoder vector, its age and its category. The state is added to the query, and training rewards the model when the same payee maps differently inside a trip window or after a reorganisation. A second arm borrows PinnerFormer's objective: train the state to predict the categories of the next two weeks. Zepto's gate between long-term and recent history is the model for mixing the two. It would be read like row 194, alone and fused with decider.

Questions it raised

  • Can a state vector learn trips and phases from synthetic households, where routing is mostly by time?
  • Does it help the owner's catch-all and person categories, where prototypes failed?

Row 199 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §178 Fair fusion and amounts

Why we ran it

Row 200 · 2026-10-05 · REAL-27, MODEL-25 · §178 Fair fusion and amounts

Treating amounts as numbers, not words

Why we ran it

The encoders read "$9.99" as text. Text pieces do not carry size: "$9.99" and "$10.00" share almost no pieces, and an exact repeat (a monthly bill) is not marked as special. The owner asked: "is representing them as strings a bad idea? ... embed 'less than $10', '$10-25' ...", and gave the case to watch: "A small amount of money at QT is likely to be a treat, not gas." This follows the fusion settings fixed in row 199.

What we tried

The arms, on encoder 1 and MaxSim: A, amount as text (today); B, no amount at all (retrained); C, B plus a log-amount kernel, so a neighbour counts more when its amount is close on a log scale (width chosen on synthetic data); D, a bonus for an exact amount match. Two arms run only if C or D shows that amounts matter: E, bucket words such as "under $10", and F, a learned numeric input. They are judged on the owner's budget with settings fixed on synthetic data, and payees the owner has already filed under two or more categories (the QT case) get their own line. The synthetic households barely route by amount; their one rule is weekday coffee under $12. A v5 generator with amount-dependent routing (gas-station snacks against fuel, small against large online orders) would let training see it.

Example prompt

QT 0412 | $4.12  | Wed   -> Treats
QT 0412 | $41.80 | Wed   -> Fuel

Arm C: neighbour weight x exp(-(log 4.12 - log 41.80)^2 / width)

Illustrative, invented amounts: the same payee at two sizes should go to two categories. Arm C makes a $41.80 neighbour count for little when the query is $4.12.
